The syllabus, as stated

The course description splits cleanly in two. That split matters for planning, because the halves want different things from you — the first is mathematical, the second is methodological.

First half — foundations of probability Events · conditional probability · independence · random variables · standard distributions · expectation and variance · joint distributions · concentration inequalities · laws of large numbers · the central limit theorem · finite-state Markov chains.
Second half — statistical inference Sampling and statistical models · estimation and likelihood · sampling distributions · bootstrap methods · confidence intervals · hypothesis and permutation tests · categorical data analysis · linear regression · model diagnostics · overfitting · cross-validation · basic binary classification.
Stated learning outcomes By the end you should be able to formulate and analyse probabilistic and statistical models, calculate and simulate probabilities, quantify uncertainty, and interpret statistical results — then select appropriate methods for estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, regression and basic classification, evaluate their assumptions and limitations, and implement reproducible analyses in Python.

Note how much of that is judgement rather than calculation: select, evaluate assumptions, interpret. That is a hint about what the exam will ask beyond computation.

Delivery is explicitly mixed: motivating questions or data examples, then formal definitions and derivations, then worked calculations or proofs, then live Python demonstrations. Simulations and real datasets are used to build intuition and to assess the methods themselves.

Assessment

One exam, everything on it There is a single written exam at the end of the lecture, covering everything presented. Your performance in that exam determines the grade — there is no continuous assessment component, no homework percentage, no project.

Two consequences worth planning around:
  • Nothing forces you to keep up. This is exactly the failure mode of your first-year courses. The fix that already works for you — a deck reviewed from week 1 — applies here unchanged.
  • "Everything covered in the lecture is relevant." Including, presumably, the live Python demonstrations and the simulation-based arguments. Do not treat the computational half as decoration.

4James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani & Taylor — An Introduction to Statistical Learning, with Applications in Python (2023)FREE PDF Regression, classification, resampling, model evaluation — the last third of the syllabus, with Python labs. This is the book for cross-validation and overfitting.

6Blitzstein & Hwang — Introduction to Probability, 2nd ed. (2019)BOOK Listed as supplementary, for conceptual explanations and exercises. The book itself is not free, but the author's Harvard course is: Stat 110 lectures on YouTube — 34 sessions, and the single best source for intuition on conditional probability and expectation.
3Devore, Berk & Carlton — Modern Mathematical Statistics with Applications, 3rd ed. (2021)SPRINGER The reference for estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing and regression. Often available through the university library's Springer access — worth checking before buying.

If you read one thing Chan. It is free, it is named the principal reference, and it is the only listed book that covers both halves in one place with the Python integration the lectures use. Add Computational and Inferential Thinking when you reach the inference half, and Stat 110 videos whenever a concept refuses to click.

A six-week head start

Six weeks × roughly 4–5 hours takes you through the whole first half before the first lecture. That is the half that is hardest to catch up on later, because everything after it depends on it.

WeekTopicDo
1Sample spaces, events, conditional probability, independenceChan ch. 1–2. Get Bayes and the law of total probability automatic — everything downstream leans on them. Stat 110 lectures 1–5 if the intuition lags.
2Random variables, PMF/PDF/CDF, expectation and varianceChan ch. 3–4. Derive $\mathbb{E}$ and $\operatorname{Var}$ for Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, Poisson, uniform, exponential, normal from the definition, not from a table.
3Joint distributions, covariance, conditional expectationChan ch. 5. The key skill is marginalising and conditioning without losing track of which variable is which.
4Concentration inequalitiesMarkov → Chebyshev → Chernoff/Hoeffding. You already know the first two from AlgoDat — see the probability toolkit. This week is mostly consolidation plus the exponential bounds.
5Laws of large numbers & the CLTChan ch. 6. Simulate both: average $n$ draws for growing $n$ and watch the histogram of the standardised mean become normal. This is where simulation earns its place.
6Finite-state Markov chainsTransition matrices, $n$-step transitions as $P^{n}$, stationary distributions as a left eigenvector. This is linear algebra — see the spectral theorem.
Then stop Do not pre-read the inference half. It is taught with the lecturer's own datasets and Python demos, and reading it cold without those is much less efficient than the first half. Arrive strong on probability, and let inference be taught.

The Python side

Python is not incidental here — the outcomes include implementing reproducible analyses, and the lectures use live demonstrations. If your Python is rusty, fixing it now is the cheapest possible preparation, and it pays into Tools in Data Science at the same time.

The minimum useful stack
  • numpy — arrays, vectorised arithmetic, numpy.random for simulation. docs
  • pandas — data frames, grouping, joins, reading CSVs. docs
  • matplotlib — histograms and scatter plots; enough to see a distribution.
  • scipy.stats — distributions, and the standard tests when you reach the inference half.
A good self-test: simulate 10 000 draws from a distribution, plot the histogram against the true density, then plot the sample mean's distribution for $n=1,5,30$ and watch the CLT happen. If you can write that unaided, your Python is sufficient.

What carries over from first year

FromCarries into
AlgoDat probability toolkit — indicators, linearity of expectation, Markov, Chebyshev, geometric distributionWeeks 1–4 almost verbatim. You have already proved Markov and derived Chebyshev from it; this course simply goes further into Chernoff-type bounds.
Spectral theorem & eigenvectorsMarkov chains: stationary distributions are eigenvectors of the transition matrix for eigenvalue 1.
Orthogonal projection & least squaresLinear regression is a projection. You have already proved the normal equations — the statistics half adds the probabilistic model on top of geometry you know.
Analysis — series, convergence, limitsLaws of large numbers and the CLT are convergence statements. Knowing what "converges in probability" versus "in distribution" means is an analysis skill.
The reassuring version Roughly a third of the first half is material you have already met from a different angle. That is why this course is the one worth starting early: your marginal hour buys more here than anywhere else in the second year.
