The rule that makes this course click

Every probability statement gets simulated, and every simulation gets a formula. The course is explicitly "mathematical theory consistently combined with computation" — so treat a result as unlearned until you have both: the derivation on paper and a short script whose Monte-Carlo estimate lands on it. When the two disagree, one of them is wrong, and finding out which is the single most instructive half-hour in the whole subject.

Unit 1 Events & probability spaces sample space · events as sets · the three axioms · counting · inclusion–exclusion · union bound
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

A probability model is a triple $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$: outcomes, the events you are allowed to ask about, and a measure with $P(A)\ge 0$, $P(\Omega)=1$, and $P(\bigcup_i A_i)=\sum_i P(A_i)$ for disjoint $A_i$. Everything else in the first half of this course is a consequence of those three lines.

Derive, don't memorise: $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$; monotonicity $A\subseteq B \Rightarrow P(A)\le P(B)$; inclusion–exclusion $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$; the union bound $P(\bigcup_i A_i)\le\sum_i P(A_i)$; and continuity $P(A_n)\to P(A)$ for $A_n\uparrow A$. Counting is a special case, not the subject: $|A|/|\Omega|$ is a valid probability only when the model declares outcomes equally likely.

🐍 Python lab — counting vs simulating 
# the birthday problem: exact formula vs Monte Carlo
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def sim(n, trials=200_000):
    days = rng.integers(0, 365, size=(trials, n))
    return np.mean([len(np.unique(r)) < n for r in days])
exact = lambda n: 1 - np.prod([(365-k)/365 for k in range(n)])
# agree to ~3 decimals at n = 23 -> 0.507
🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

The union bound is the single most-used inequality in the rest of the course and in machine learning: it is how a guarantee for one hypothesis becomes a guarantee for all of them at once, and it costs nothing but a factor of the number of events.

The classic mistake

Assuming equally likely outcomes because the problem mentions dice or cards. Write $\Omega$ down explicitly: "two dice" has 36 ordered outcomes, not 21 unordered ones, and the two models give different answers.

🗣 Before moving on
Without notes: (1) state the three axioms and derive $P(A\cup B)$ from them; (2) why does $P$ need a $\sigma$-algebra rather than every subset of $\Omega$? (3) give a sample space where $|A|/|\Omega|$ is the wrong answer, and say why.
Unit 2 Conditional probability, Bayes & independence conditioning · multiplication rule · law of total probability · Bayes · independence vs conditional independence · base rates
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

$P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$ for $P(B)\gt 0$ — and note that $P(\cdot\mid B)$ is itself a probability measure, so every result from unit 1 still holds inside it. From it: the multiplication rule $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$, the law of total probability $P(A)=\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)$ over a partition, and Bayes: $P(B_i\mid A)=\dfrac{P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)}{\sum_j P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}$.

Independence is $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$, equivalently $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ — an assumption of the model, never something you read off a data table. Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence, and conditional independence given $C$ neither implies nor is implied by independence.

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

Every classifier reports $P(\text{class}\mid\text{features})$ and is trained on $P(\text{features}\mid\text{class})$ — naive Bayes is literally this unit plus a conditional-independence assumption. Precision, recall and the confusion matrix in unit 16 are conditional probabilities read in the two different directions.

The classic mistake

Swapping $P(A\mid B)$ for $P(B\mid A)$ — the prosecutor's fallacy. Say the conditioning event out loud every time: "given that the test is positive", not "given the disease".

🗣 Before moving on
Without notes: (1) derive Bayes' rule from the definition of conditional probability; (2) give three events that are pairwise but not mutually independent; (3) explain in one sentence why a 99%-accurate test for a rare disease mostly returns false positives.
Unit 3 Discrete random variables PMF & CDF · Bernoulli · binomial · geometric · negative binomial · Poisson · hypergeometric · the Poisson limit
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

A random variable is a function $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$; its PMF $p_X(k)=P(X=k)$ and CDF $F_X(x)=P(X\le x)$ are what you actually compute with. Learn each named model by its story, not its formula: binomial = number of successes in $n$ independent trials; geometric = trials until the first success; Poisson = counts of rare events at rate $\lambda$; hypergeometric = successes when sampling without replacement.

Two results worth deriving in full: the Poisson limit, $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\to e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$ when $n\to\infty$, $np\to\lambda$; and memorylessness of the geometric, $P(X\gt m+n\mid X\gt m)=P(X\gt n)$. Sums of independent binomials with the same $p$ are binomial; sums of independent Poissons are Poisson.

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

Click-through, conversion and A/B test data are binomial; arrivals, defects and word counts are Poisson; "how many attempts until it works" is geometric. Choosing the wrong story is how a plausible-looking analysis becomes silently wrong.

The classic mistake

Binomial where hypergeometric belongs. Sampling without replacement from a small population makes trials dependent; the mean survives but the variance shrinks by the finite-population correction.

🗣 Before moving on
Without notes: (1) state the story behind each of the five named discrete models; (2) prove the geometric is memoryless; (3) when does a binomial become a Poisson, and what does "rare" mean quantitatively?
Unit 4 Continuous random variables PDF & CDF · uniform · exponential · normal · gamma & beta · transformations · inverse-transform sampling · quantiles
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

$F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t)\,dt$ and $f_X=F_X'$ where $F_X$ is differentiable. A density is not a probability: $f_X(x)$ can exceed 1, and $P(X=x)=0$ for every single point — probability lives in areas, so $P(a\le X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a)$ and the endpoints do not matter.

Transformations are the workhorse. For strictly monotone $g$, $f_Y(y)=f_X(g^{-1}(y))\,\bigl|\tfrac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\bigr|$; when $g$ is not monotone, split the domain and add the branches. Two consequences you will use all term: standardisation $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma$, and inverse-transform sampling — if $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ then $F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$, which is how a computer produces any distribution at all.

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

Every simulation in the rest of this programme rests on inverse-transform (or rejection) sampling, and every normal-approximation argument from unit 8 onwards rests on standardisation. The Q–Q plot you build here is the diagnostic you will reuse on regression residuals in unit 15.

The classic mistake

Reading a density as a probability, and forgetting the Jacobian $|dg^{-1}/dy|$ when transforming. If your transformed "density" does not integrate to 1, that factor is what you dropped.

Unit 5 Expectation, variance & moments expectation · LOTUS · linearity · variance & standard deviation · indicator trick · moment-generating functions · Jensen
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

LOTUS — you do not need the distribution of $Y=g(X)$ to get its mean: $E[g(X)]=\sum_k g(k)p_X(k)$ or $\int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx$. Linearity $E[aX+bY]=aE[X]+bE[Y]$ holds with no independence assumption whatsoever, which is what makes the indicator trick work: write $X=\sum_i \mathbb{1}_{A_i}$ and the mean falls out in one line even when the $A_i$ are hopelessly dependent.

Variance is not linear: $\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2$, $\mathrm{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)$, and $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)$ only when $X,Y$ are uncorrelated. The MGF $M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]$ generates moments by differentiation at $0$, turns sums of independent variables into products, and determines the distribution when it exists near $0$ — it is the tool behind both the Chernoff bound (unit 7) and the CLT (unit 8).

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

Every loss function is an expectation, and the bias–variance decomposition of unit 16 is this unit's algebra applied to prediction error. "Standard error" is nothing but $\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)}=\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, which you derive here and use for the rest of the course.

The classic mistake

Assuming $E[g(X)]=g(E[X])$. Jensen says which way the inequality goes for convex $g$, and the gap is exactly why an unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2$ does not give an unbiased estimator of $\sigma$.

🗣 Before moving on
Without notes: (1) get the mean of a binomial in one line with indicators; (2) state LOTUS and say why it saves work; (3) differentiate the Poisson MGF twice to recover $E[X]$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X)$.
Unit 6 Joint distributions & conditional expectation joint & marginal · independence · covariance & correlation · covariance matrix · $E[Y\mid X]$ · tower property · law of total variance · bivariate normal · convolution
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

Marginals come from summing or integrating out; independence is the statement that the joint factors, $f_{X,Y}=f_Xf_Y$. Dependence is measured by $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]$ and $\rho=\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)\in[-1,1]$, giving $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$. Uncorrelated does not mean independent — $X\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-1,1)$ with $Y=X^2$ is the example to remember — though for jointly normal variables the two coincide.

$E[Y\mid X]$ is a random variable, a function of $X$: the tower property $E\bigl[E[Y\mid X]\bigr]=E[Y]$ and the law of total variance $\mathrm{Var}(Y)=E[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)]+\mathrm{Var}(E[Y\mid X])$ are the two identities to have automatic. For the bivariate normal, $E[Y\mid X=x]=\mu_Y+\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X)$ — a straight line, which is precisely the regression line of unit 15.

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

The covariance matrix is the object PCA diagonalises, and $E[Y\mid X]$ is what every regression model is trying to estimate — least squares is the best approximation to it inside a linear family. The law of total variance is the honest version of "explained versus unexplained variation".

The classic mistake

Reading $\rho=0$ as "no relationship". Correlation only sees the linear part; a perfect parabola has zero correlation. Plot the data before you trust a coefficient.

Unit 7 Concentration inequalities Markov · Chebyshev · Chernoff via the MGF · Hoeffding · sample-size bounds · what "with high probability" means
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

Three bounds, each buying sharpness with an extra assumption. Markov (only $X\ge 0$ and a mean): $P(X\ge a)\le E[X]/a$. Chebyshev (a variance too), obtained by applying Markov to $(X-\mu)^2$: $P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le 1/k^{2}$. Chernoff / Hoeffding (an MGF, or bounded variables): for iid $X_i\in[a,b]$,

$P\bigl(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge t\bigr)\ \le\ 2\exp\!\Bigl(-\dfrac{2nt^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}\Bigr).$

The pattern is always the same: turn a tail into an expectation, then optimise the free parameter. Read the last bound backwards and it sizes an experiment — $n\ge\frac{(b-a)^2}{2t^2}\log\frac{2}{\delta}$ samples put you within $t$ of the truth with probability $1-\delta$, with no distributional assumption at all.

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

Concentration is what licenses learning from finite data: a test-set estimate of accuracy is a mean of bounded variables, so Hoeffding plus a union bound over models is a generalisation guarantee. It is also how you answer "how many samples do I need?" before collecting any.

The classic mistake

Treating a bound as an estimate. These are worst-case guarantees; Chebyshev at $k=2$ promises only 75% coverage while a normal delivers 95%. Use bounds to be safe, the CLT to be accurate.

🗣 Before moving on
Without notes: (1) prove Markov, then get Chebyshev from it; (2) state Hoeffding and name every assumption it needs; (3) how many coin flips to estimate $p$ within $\pm 0.01$ with 95% confidence — by Hoeffding, and by the CLT?
Unit 8 Laws of large numbers & the central limit theorem convergence in probability / almost surely / in distribution · WLLN & SLLN · the CLT · $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ · normal approximation · when it fails
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

Two different promises about $\bar X_n$. The law of large numbers says where it goes: $\bar X_n\to\mu$ (in probability for the weak law — one line from Chebyshev — and almost surely for the strong law). The central limit theorem says how it wobbles on the way:

$\dfrac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ N(0,1)$  for iid $X_i$ with finite variance.

Read it as: the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$ and its shape is normal whatever the population looked like. That is what makes $\bar X\pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ meaningful in unit 12. The hypotheses matter: finite variance is essential (a Cauchy sample mean never settles), independence is essential, and for skewed populations or small $n$ the approximation can be poor — check it rather than assume it.

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

Every standard error, $t$-test, $z$-interval and A/B test in the second half of this course is the CLT wearing different clothes. It is also why quadrupling your data only halves your error bar — a fact worth knowing before promising anyone a more precise result.

The classic mistake

"$n\ge 30$, therefore normal." That rule of thumb is about the mean, never about individual observations, and it fails badly for heavy tails or strong skew. Simulate the sampling distribution and look.

Unit 9 Finite-state Markov chains the Markov property · transition matrix · $n$-step probabilities · irreducibility & aperiodicity · stationary distribution · absorption · reversibility
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

The Markov property is a statement about forgetting: $P(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i, \text{past})=P(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i)=p_{ij}$. Collect the $p_{ij}$ into a row-stochastic matrix $P$ and all the dynamics become linear algebra: $n$-step probabilities are the entries of $P^{n}$ (Chapman–Kolmogorov), and a distribution evolves as the row vector $\pi_{t+1}=\pi_t P$.

A stationary distribution solves $\pi P=\pi$ with $\pi_i\ge 0$, $\sum_i\pi_i=1$ — a left eigenvector of $P$ for eigenvalue 1. For a finite chain that is irreducible (every state reachable from every other) and aperiodic, it exists, is unique, and $\pi_t\to\pi$ from any start. Two more tools carry most exam questions: first-step analysis for absorption probabilities and expected hitting times, and detailed balance $\pi_ip_{ij}=\pi_jp_{ji}$ as a shortcut to $\pi$ when the chain is reversible.

pi_pow = np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 200)[0]
# 2. left eigenvector for eigenvalue 1
w, v = np.linalg.eig(P.T)
pi_eig = np.real(v[:, np.argmin(abs(w-1))]); pi_eig /= pi_eig.sum()
# 3. one long trajectory -- the ergodic theorem, empirically
s, visits = 0, np.zeros(3)
for _ in range(500_000):
    visits[s] += 1; s = rng.choice(3, p=P[s])
# all three agree to ~3 decimals
🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random surfer; MCMC turns the convergence theorem around, designing a chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior you want to sample. Queueing, reliability and text generation are all finite-state chains before they are anything else.

The classic mistake

Solving $\pi P=\pi$ and forgetting $\sum_i\pi_i=1$ — the eigenvector is only determined up to scale. And a chain can be irreducible yet periodic, in which case $\pi$ exists but $\pi_t$ never converges; it cycles.

Unit 10 Sampling & statistical models population vs sample · the iid model · statistic vs parameter · sampling distribution · $\bar X$ and $S^2$ · why $n-1$ · sampling bias
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

The second half of the course runs the first half backwards. Before: the model was given and you computed probabilities. Now: the data are given and the model's parameter $\theta$ is unknown. A statistical model says the data are $X_1,\dots,X_n$ iid from $f(\cdot\,;\theta)$; a statistic is any function of the data alone; its distribution over repeated samples is the sampling distribution, and every claim about uncertainty in this course is a statement about that distribution.

Core facts: $E[\bar X]=\mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$ always; $S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$ — the $n-1$ is the one degree of freedom spent estimating $\mu$, not a fudge. And no amount of data fixes a broken sampling scheme: bias does not shrink with $n$, it converges to the wrong answer more confidently.

Unit 11 Estimation & likelihood method of moments · likelihood & log-likelihood · MLE · bias, variance, MSE · consistency · Fisher information · asymptotic normality
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

The likelihood $L(\theta)=\prod_i f(x_i;\theta)$ is the joint density read as a function of $\theta$ with the data held fixed — not a probability distribution over $\theta$. Maximising $\ell(\theta)=\log L(\theta)$ gives the MLE; the log turns products into sums, and the sum is what you actually differentiate.

Judge an estimator by its whole error, not its centre: $\mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta)=\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)+\mathrm{bias}(\hat\theta)^2$. Unbiased is not automatically better — a little bias for a lot less variance is the whole idea behind regularisation in unit 16. Know the standard results: $\hat p=\bar X$ for Bernoulli, $\hat\lambda=\bar X$ for Poisson, $\hat\mu=\bar X$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$ (biased!) for the normal, and $\hat\theta=\max_i x_i$ for $\mathrm{Unif}(0,\theta)$ — the case where calculus fails and you must argue directly. Asymptotically the MLE is consistent and $\hat\theta\approx N\bigl(\theta, 1/(nI(\theta))\bigr)$, which is where its standard errors come from.

Unit 12 Sampling distributions, the bootstrap & confidence intervals $\chi^2$, $t$ and $F$ · $z$- vs $t$-intervals · CI for a mean and a proportion · coverage · the bootstrap · sample size
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

Three derived distributions, each with a job. If $X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ iid then $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$, and replacing the unknown $\sigma$ by $S$ turns the $z$ into $t$: $\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt{n}}\sim t_{n-1}$ — fatter tails, paying for the estimated scale. The $F$ is a ratio of two scaled $\chi^2$s and shows up comparing variances and in regression.

A confidence interval inverts a sampling distribution: $\bar x\pm t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}\,s/\sqrt n$. Its guarantee is about the procedure, not this interval — 95% of intervals built this way over repeated samples contain $\theta$; the one on your page either does or does not. When no formula exists (a median, a correlation, an $R^2$), the bootstrap gets you a sampling distribution anyway: resample $n$ points from your data with replacement, recompute the statistic, repeat $B$ times, and read off the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. It needs the sample to represent the population and the statistic to be smooth — it fails for extremes like the maximum.

Unit 13 Hypothesis & permutation tests $H_0$ vs $H_1$ · null distribution · $p$-values · type I/II errors & power · $z$- and $t$-tests · permutation tests · multiple testing
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

A test is one question asked in a fixed order: state $H_0$ and $H_1$, choose a statistic $T$, work out the distribution of $T$ assuming $H_0$ is true, and ask how extreme the observed $t_{\text{obs}}$ is in that distribution. The $p$-value is $P(T\text{ at least as extreme as }t_{\text{obs}}\mid H_0)$ — a statement about data given the hypothesis, never about the hypothesis given the data.

Two error types, and they trade off: type I (reject a true $H_0$, rate $\alpha$) and type II (fail to reject a false $H_0$, rate $\beta$); power $=1-\beta$ rises with $n$, with effect size, and with $\alpha$. Tests and intervals are two views of one object: a two-sided level-$\alpha$ test rejects $\theta_0$ exactly when the $1-\alpha$ confidence interval excludes it. When you cannot justify a parametric null, build the null distribution yourself: a permutation test shuffles the group labels thousands of times, because under "the groups are alike" every labelling was equally likely.

# two groups, one statistic, no distributional assumption at all
a, b = rng.normal(0, 1, 40), rng.normal(0.6, 1, 35)

🧠 Why it matters
Where it shows up

Every A/B test and every "is model B better than model A?" comparison. Permutation tests in particular travel everywhere, because they work for any statistic you can compute — median difference, AUC gap, whatever the situation actually calls for.

The classic mistake

Reading $p$ as "the probability $H_0$ is true", and reading $p\gt 0.05$ as "no effect" when it may just mean no power. Twenty tests at $\alpha=0.05$ produce one significant result on average from pure noise — say how many tests you ran.

Unit 14 Categorical data analysis contingency tables · goodness of fit · the $\chi^2$ test of independence · expected counts & degrees of freedom · Fisher's exact test · odds ratios
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

One statistic covers both jobs: $\chi^2=\sum\dfrac{(O-E)^2}{E}$, summed over cells. For goodness of fit, $E_i=np_i$ under the claimed distribution and $\mathrm{df}=k-1-(\text{parameters estimated})$. For independence in an $r\times c$ table, the null builds the expected counts from the margins, $E_{ij}=\dfrac{R_i C_j}{n}$, and $\mathrm{df}=(r-1)(c-1)$ — the count of free cells once the margins are fixed.

The $\chi^2$ reference distribution is an approximation: it needs independent observations, counts (not percentages), and expected cell counts of roughly 5 or more. When cells are sparse, use Fisher's exact test or permute. And a significant $\chi^2$ says only "not independent" — for the size and direction of the association you need residuals, a relative risk, or an odds ratio $\dfrac{a d}{b c}$.

Unit 15 Linear regression & model diagnostics the linear model · least squares · coefficient interpretation · $R^2$ · inference on $\beta$ · residual plots · leverage & influence · multicollinearity
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

The model is $Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{i1}+\dots+\beta_px_{ip}+\varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i$ iid, mean zero, constant variance $\sigma^2$ — and normal if you want exact $t$-tests. Least squares minimises $\mathrm{RSS}=\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2$, giving $\hat\beta=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y$: geometrically, the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space of $X$, which is exactly the projection you met in linear algebra.

Read the output properly. $\hat\beta_j$ is the expected change in $Y$ per unit of $x_j$ with the other predictors held fixed; $R^2$ is the fraction of variance explained and never decreases when you add a predictor, so it cannot be used to choose models; each coefficient's $t=\hat\beta_j/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$ tests $\beta_j=0$ given the rest. The assumptions live in the residuals, not in $R^2$: residuals vs fitted (linearity, equal variance), a Q–Q plot (normality), leverage and Cook's distance (single points steering the fit). Anscombe's quartet is the standing reminder that four wildly different datasets can share every summary number.

Unit 16 Overfitting, cross-validation & binary classification train/test · bias–variance · regularisation · $K$-fold CV · data leakage · logistic regression · confusion matrix · precision/recall · ROC & AUC
Tap lanes as you finish each pass
The spine of this unit

Training error falls forever as a model grows; test error falls, bottoms out, then climbs. That U is the bias–variance trade-off of unit 11 applied to prediction — and it is why the error you report must come from data the model never saw. $K$-fold cross-validation reuses the data honestly: split into $K$ parts, train on $K-1$, test on the held-out one, rotate, average. The rule that catches everybody: every fitted step — scaling, imputation, feature selection — must happen inside the fold, or the estimate leaks and flatters itself.

Logistic regression is unit 11 again with a Bernoulli likelihood: model the log-odds as linear, $\log\frac{p}{1-p}=\beta^{\top}x$, so $p=\sigma(\beta^{\top}x)$, and fit by maximum likelihood (equivalently, minimise cross-entropy). It outputs a probability; a threshold turns it into a decision, and the threshold is a business choice, not a statistical one. Judge it with the confusion matrix and its ratios — precision $\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$, recall $\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$ — and with the ROC curve and its AUC, which sweeps every threshold at once. On imbalanced data accuracy is worthless: 99% of nothing is the majority class.

scipy.stats uses location and scale, not the parameters in your lecture notes. Where marks are actually lost
  • Unstated assumptions. Independence, finite variance, expected counts $\ge5$ — name them, even when the question does not ask.
  • Interpretation sentences. "95% confidence" and "$p$-value" have precise meanings; a fluent-but-wrong sentence costs the whole part.
