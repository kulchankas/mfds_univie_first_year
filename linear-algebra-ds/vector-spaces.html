Vector spaces, projections & symmetries

Chapter 1 is the vocabulary the rest of the book speaks. Most of it is definitions you can absorb quickly — except projections and symmetries, which trip almost everyone up, because a Chapter-1 projection is not the orthogonal projection you will meet in Chapter 2. That section below is the long one, deliberately — and the real exam opened with a projection question worth 5 of its 24 points, so it earns the space.

Vector spaces & subspaces

A vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ (for us $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$) is a set with addition and scalar multiplication satisfying the usual eight axioms. In practice you almost never verify all eight — you verify that something sits inside a known space, i.e. that it is a subspace.

The subspace test $U\subseteq V$ is a subspace iff (1) $0\in U$; (2) $u,w\in U\Rightarrow u+w\in U$; (3) $\lambda\in\mathbb{F},u\in U\Rightarrow\lambda u\in U$.

Conditions (2) and (3) combine into one: $u,w\in U,\ \lambda\in\mathbb{F}\Rightarrow u+\lambda w\in U$.
Use (1) as your disproof tool To show something is not a subspace, check $0$ first — it is one line and it usually works. $\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_ix_i=1\}$ fails immediately. Similarly $\{x:x_1\ge0\}$ is closed under addition but not under $\lambda=-1$, and the union of the two coordinate axes in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is closed under scalars but not addition.
Intersection yes, union no An arbitrary intersection of subspaces is a subspace. A union almost never is: $U\cup W$ is a subspace iff $U\subseteq W$ or $W\subseteq U$. The correct replacement for union is the sum $$U+W=\{u+w:u\in U,\ w\in W\},$$ which is the smallest subspace containing both.

Span, independence, basis, dimension

The four definitions
  • Span $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_k)$ — all linear combinations $\sum\lambda_iv_i$. Always a subspace.
  • Linearly independent — $\sum\lambda_iv_i=0$ forces every $\lambda_i=0$.
  • Basis — independent and spanning.
  • Dimension — the common cardinality of every basis.
The characterisation to actually use $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ is a basis of $V$ iff every $v\in V$ has exactly one representation $v=\sum\lambda_iv_i$. Spanning gives at least one; independence gives at most one. That "unique decomposition" phrasing is what makes direct sums and projections work below — keep it in mind.
Facts you will use without proof In a space of dimension $n$: any independent list has $\le n$ vectors; any spanning list has $\ge n$; any independent list of exactly $n$ vectors is a basis, and so is any spanning list of exactly $n$. Every independent list extends to a basis; every spanning list contains one. For $U\subseteq V$: $\dim U\le\dim V$, with equality iff $U=V$.

Direct sums

Definition $V=U\oplus W$ ("$V$ is the direct sum of $U$ and $W$", and $W$ is a complement of $U$) when $$V=U+W\quad\text{and}\quad U\cap W=\{0\}.$$ Equivalently — and this is the version that matters — every $v\in V$ writes uniquely as $v=u+w$ with $u\in U$, $w\in W$.

Why the two versions agree $V=U+W$ gives existence. For uniqueness, suppose $u_1+w_1=u_2+w_2$. Then $u_1-u_2=w_2-w_1$ lies in both $U$ and $W$, hence is $0$ — so $u_1=u_2$ and $w_1=w_2$. Conversely, if decompositions are unique then any $v\in U\cap W$ has the two decompositions $v+0$ and $0+v$, forcing $v=0$.

Dimension In general $$\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W),$$ so for a direct sum the correction term vanishes: $$\dim(U\oplus W)=\dim U+\dim W .$$ Concatenating a basis of $U$ with a basis of $W$ gives a basis of $U\oplus W$ — an adapted basis, which we use constantly below.
Complements exist but are wildly non-unique Every subspace $U\subseteq V$ has some complement (extend a basis of $U$ to a basis of $V$). It has many. In $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, take $U$ = the line $y=x$. Then the $y$-axis is a complement, the $x$-axis is a complement, and so is every line through the origin other than $U$ itself.

This is the root of the projection confusion: "project onto $U$" is meaningless until you say along which complement. Chapter 2 removes the ambiguity by always choosing $W=U^{\perp}$ — but that requires an inner product, which Chapter 1 does not have.

★ Projections

This is the section to slow down on.

Definition A linear map $p:V\to V$ is a projection (projector) if it is idempotent: $$p\circ p=p .$$ That is the whole definition. No inner product, no orthogonality, no angles — just $p^{2}=p$.
What idempotence means "Doing it twice is the same as doing it once." Once you have landed in the target, applying $p$ again moves nothing. So the image consists exactly of the points $p$ leaves alone — the fixed points. That single sentence generates every property below.

The structure theorem

Theorem — projections ↔ direct sums If $p:V\to V$ is a projection then $$V=\operatorname{im}p\;\oplus\;\ker p,$$ and $p$ is precisely "decompose $v=u+w$ along this sum, then keep $u$". Moreover $$\operatorname{im}p=\ker(p-\operatorname{id})=\{v\in V:p(v)=v\}.$$ Conversely, every decomposition $V=U\oplus W$ defines a projection $p(u+w)=u$, with $\operatorname{im}p=U$ and $\ker p=W$.

Proof — the sum is everything For any $v$ write $$v=\underbrace{p(v)}_{\in\,\operatorname{im}p}+\underbrace{\big(v-p(v)\big)}_{\in\,\ker p},$$ and the second piece really is in the kernel because $$p\big(v-p(v)\big)=p(v)-p^{2}(v)=p(v)-p(v)=0 .$$ So $V=\operatorname{im}p+\ker p$.

Proof — the intersection is trivial Let $v\in\operatorname{im}p\cap\ker p$. Being in the image, $v=p(u)$ for some $u$. Then $$v=p(u)=p^{2}(u)=p\big(p(u)\big)=p(v)=0,$$ the last step because $v\in\ker p$. Hence $\operatorname{im}p\cap\ker p=\{0\}$ and the sum is direct. ∎

Proof — image = fixed points If $p(v)=v$ then obviously $v\in\operatorname{im}p$. Conversely if $v\in\operatorname{im}p$, say $v=p(u)$, then $p(v)=p^{2}(u)=p(u)=v$. ∎

The practical consequence To identify a projection you never need clever algebra. The image is where you land — solve $p(v)=v$. The kernel is the direction you project along — solve $p(v)=0$. Those two subspaces determine $p$ completely.

The complementary projection

$q=\operatorname{id}-p$ If $p$ is a projection then so is $q=\operatorname{id}-p$, because $$q^{2}=(\operatorname{id}-p)^{2}=\operatorname{id}-2p+p^{2}=\operatorname{id}-2p+p=\operatorname{id}-p=q .$$ It projects the other way: $\operatorname{im}q=\ker p$ and $\ker q=\operatorname{im}p$. And $p$ and $q$ annihilate each other: $$pq=qp=0,\qquad p+q=\operatorname{id}.$$

Matrix form — why an adapted basis is worth the trouble

Let $V=U\oplus W$ with $\dim U=r$, $\dim V=n$. Pick a basis $(u_1,\dots,u_r)$ of $U$ and $(w_1,\dots,w_{n-r})$ of $W$, and use the concatenation as a basis of $V$. In that basis the projection onto $U$ along $W$ is $$[p]=\begin{pmatrix}I_r&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ So every projection looks like this in a suitable basis — which instantly gives:

  • $\operatorname{rank}p=\dim U=r$ and $\operatorname{tr}(p)=r$. So for a projection, trace = rank. A quick sanity check on any exercise.
  • The only eigenvalues are $1$ (on $U$) and $0$ (on $W$), and $p$ is always diagonalizable.
  • $\det p=0$ unless $p=\operatorname{id}$.

Worked example — the same line, two different projections

Work in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and let $U=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1)\}$, the line $y=x$. We project onto $U$ twice, along two different complements, and get two different maps.

(a) Along the $y$-axis Take $W=\operatorname{span}\{(0,1)\}$. Decompose $(x,y)=a(1,1)+b(0,1)$: comparing coordinates, $a=x$ and $a+b=y$, so $b=y-x$. Keeping the $U$-part, $$p(x,y)=x\,(1,1)=(x,x),\qquad [p]=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ Check idempotence: $p(p(x,y))=p(x,x)=(x,x)$ ✓. Check trace $=1+0=1=\dim U$ ✓.

(b) Along the perpendicular Take $W=U^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1)\}$. Decompose $(x,y)=a(1,1)+b(1,-1)$: then $a+b=x$, $a-b=y$, so $a=\tfrac{x+y}{2}$. Hence $$P(x,y)=\frac{x+y}{2}(1,1),\qquad [P]=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$ Again $P^{2}=P$ ✓ and $\operatorname{tr}P=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$ ✓.

The distinction that causes the trouble Both $p$ and $P$ are perfectly valid projections onto the same line $U$. They differ only in the complement. Notice:
$[p]$ — along the $y$-axis$[P]$ — orthogonal
Idempotent $M^{2}=M$yesyes
Symmetric $M^{\mathsf T}=M$noyes
Shortest distance to $U$?noyes
Idempotent = projection. Idempotent and self-adjoint = orthogonal projection. Chapter 1 gives you the first; Chapter 2 adds an inner product and picks out the second. When a Chapter-1 exercise says "projection", it does not mean the orthogonal one unless it says so.

★ Symmetries

Definition A linear map $s:V\to V$ is a symmetry (an involution) if $$s\circ s=\operatorname{id}.$$ So $s$ is its own inverse. Geometrically: a reflection.
Theorem — symmetries ↔ direct sums too If $s^{2}=\operatorname{id}$ then $$V=\ker(s-\operatorname{id})\;\oplus\;\ker(s+\operatorname{id}),$$ i.e. $V$ splits into the vectors $s$ fixes ($s(v)=v$) and the vectors it flips ($s(v)=-v$). These are the eigenspaces for $+1$ and $-1$.

Proof The trick is to average. For any $v$, $$v=\underbrace{\frac{v+s(v)}{2}}_{=:a}+\underbrace{\frac{v-s(v)}{2}}_{=:b}.$$ Apply $s$ and use $s^{2}=\operatorname{id}$: $$s(a)=\frac{s(v)+s^{2}(v)}{2}=\frac{s(v)+v}{2}=a,\qquad s(b)=\frac{s(v)-v}{2}=-b .$$ So $a$ is fixed and $b$ is flipped, giving $V=\ker(s-\operatorname{id})+\ker(s+\operatorname{id})$. For directness, if $v$ is in both then $v=s(v)=-v$, so $2v=0$ and $v=0$. ∎

Where the $\tfrac12$ comes from That proof divides by $2$, so it needs $2\ne0$ in the field — fine over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$, false in characteristic $2$. Every statement on this page linking projections and symmetries carries that hypothesis silently.

The dictionary

Projections and symmetries are the same information $$\boxed{\;s=2p-\operatorname{id}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad p=\tfrac12\left(s+\operatorname{id}\right)\;}$$ If $p$ is the projection onto $U$ along $W$, then $s$ is the symmetry fixing $U$, flipping $W$ — and vice versa.

Check one direction Suppose $p^{2}=p$ and set $s=2p-\operatorname{id}$. Then $$s^{2}=(2p-\operatorname{id})^{2}=4p^{2}-4p+\operatorname{id}=4p-4p+\operatorname{id}=\operatorname{id}. ✓$$

And the other Suppose $s^{2}=\operatorname{id}$ and set $p=\tfrac12(s+\operatorname{id})$. Then $$p^{2}=\tfrac14\left(s^{2}+2s+\operatorname{id}\right)=\tfrac14\left(\operatorname{id}+2s+\operatorname{id}\right)=\tfrac12\left(s+\operatorname{id}\right)=p. ✓$$

Read it geometrically $p(v)$ walks $v$ half way — onto $U$. $s(v)$ walks it all the way through — to the mirror image on the far side. Hence the factor $2$: $s=2p-\operatorname{id}$ says "go to the projection, then go the same distance again".

Continuing the worked example

From the two projections above, $s=2p-\operatorname{id}$ gives two symmetries:

(a) $\displaystyle [s]=2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}$. Check: $$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2-2&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} ✓$$ A skew reflection — it fixes the line $y=x$ and flips the $y$-axis, but it is not a mirror reflection in the everyday sense.

(b) $\displaystyle [S]=2\cdot\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ — the map $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$. That is exactly the mirror reflection in the line $y=x$, which is what you would have drawn by hand. Note $[S]$ is symmetric, matching the orthogonal case.

Both fix $U$ pointwise and reverse their respective complements. Trace check: $\operatorname{tr}(s)=\operatorname{tr}(S)=0=\dim U-\dim W=1-1$ ✓ — in general $\operatorname{tr}(s)=\dim\ker(s-\operatorname{id})-\dim\ker(s+\operatorname{id})$.

★ Stable (invariant) subspaces

This was question 1 of the real exam, for 5 of 24 points The first-date paper asked: for a projection $p$ and any $f$, show that $p\circ f=f\circ p$ iff $\operatorname{im}p$ and $\ker p$ are stable by $f$. It combines everything above — so it is worth having the vocabulary straight.
Definition A subspace $U\subseteq E$ is stable (or invariant) under $f\in\mathcal{L}(E)$ if $$f(U)\subseteq U,$$ i.e. $u\in U\Rightarrow f(u)\in U$. Note the inclusion is one-way — $f(U)=U$ is not required, and is false in general (take $f=0$).
Why anyone cares If $U$ is stable, $f$ restricts to an endomorphism $f|_U\in\mathcal{L}(U)$. And if $E=U\oplus W$ with both summands stable, then in a basis adapted to the decomposition the matrix of $f$ is block diagonal: $$[f]=\begin{pmatrix}[f|_U]&0\\0&[f|_W]\end{pmatrix}.$$ Splitting a map into independent blocks is the entire point — it is what diagonalization (Ch 5) and the spectral theorem (Ch 6) do, in the special case where the blocks are one-dimensional.

Examples you should be able to produce

  • $\{0\}$ and $E$ are stable under every $f$ — the trivial ones.
  • Every eigenspace $F_\lambda=\ker(f-\lambda\,\mathrm{Id})$ is stable under $f$: if $f(x)=\lambda x$ then $f(x)=\lambda x\in F_\lambda$. More generally $\ker f$ and $\operatorname{im}f$ are stable under $f$ itself.
  • $\ker g$ and $\operatorname{im}g$ are stable under any $f$ that commutes with $g$ — that is exactly the exam question, in the case $g=p$.
The exam result Let $p$ be a projection and $f\in\mathcal{L}(E)$. Then $$p\circ f=f\circ p\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{im}p\ \text{and}\ \ker p\ \text{are stable by }f.$$

(⇒) The easy direction Assume they commute.

  • Image. Take $y\in\operatorname{im}p$, say $y=p(x)$. Then $f(y)=f(p(x))=p(f(x))\in\operatorname{im}p$. ✓
  • Kernel. Take $x\in\ker p$. Then $p(f(x))=f(p(x))=f(0)=0$, so $f(x)\in\ker p$. ✓

Both are one line. Notice neither used that $p$ is a projection — the forward direction works for any $g$ in place of $p$.

(⇐) The direction that needs the projection structure This is where the two facts from the projections section earn their keep:

  • (A) $E=\operatorname{im}p\oplus\ker p$ — so every $x$ splits uniquely as $x=y+z$;
  • (B) $p\big|_{\operatorname{im}p}=\mathrm{Id}$ — the image is the set of fixed points.

Set $g=f\circ p-p\circ f$ and show $g=0$. Split $x=y+z$ with $y\in\operatorname{im}p$, $z\in\ker p$: $$g(x)=f\underbrace{(p(y))}_{\text{(B)}\,=\,y}+f\underbrace{(p(z))}_{=\,0}-p\underbrace{(f(y))}_{\in\,\operatorname{im}p\ \text{by stability}}-p\underbrace{(f(z))}_{\in\,\ker p\ \text{by stability}}=f(y)+0-f(y)-0=0 .$$ Each of the four terms dies for a different reason — annotate which, as above. ∎

The exam-technique takeaway Whenever a question says "show $X$ is stable", the proof is always the same shape: take an element of $X$, apply $f$, show the result is still in $X$ — using whatever characterisation of $X$ is cheapest. For $\operatorname{im}p$ use "it is $p$ of something"; for $\ker p$ use "$p$ of it is zero"; for an eigenspace use the eigenvalue equation.

How to solve the exercises

Given a matrix $M$ — is it a projection? Which one?
  1. Test $M^{2}=M$. If it fails, it is not a projection; stop.
  2. Find the image $U$: solve $Mv=v$, i.e. $\ker(M-I)$. (Equivalently take the column space — but the fixed-point equation is usually faster and less error-prone.)
  3. Find the direction $W$: solve $Mv=0$, i.e. $\ker M$.
  4. Report: "$M$ is the projection onto $U$ along $W$." Sanity check $\dim U+\dim W=n$ and $\operatorname{tr}M=\dim U$.
  5. Orthogonal? Only if additionally $M^{\mathsf T}=M$ (real) or $M^{*}=M$ (complex).
Given a matrix $S$ — is it a symmetry?
  1. Test $S^{2}=I$.
  2. The mirror is $U=\ker(S-I)$ (fixed vectors); the flipped part is $W=\ker(S+I)$.
  3. Report: "$S$ is the symmetry with respect to $U$ along $W$."
  4. The associated projection is $p=\tfrac12(S+I)$; check $\operatorname{tr}S=\dim U-\dim W$.
Given $V=U\oplus W$ — build the projection
  1. Take a basis $(u_1,\dots,u_r)$ of $U$ and $(w_1,\dots,w_{n-r})$ of $W$.
  2. Solve for the coefficients: write a general $v$ as $\sum a_iu_i+\sum b_jw_j$ — a linear system.
  3. $p(v)=\sum a_iu_i$ (discard the $b_j$ part).
  4. Read off the matrix column by column: column $k$ is $p(e_k)$ in the standard basis.
Shortcut for the orthogonal case only (Chapter 2): $P=A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}$ with the basis of $U$ in the columns of $A$.
Why data science cares Projections are the geometry of fitting. Least squares is a projection onto the column space of the design matrix; the "hat matrix" $H=X(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}$ that turns $y$ into $\hat y$ is exactly an orthogonal projection, and $\operatorname{tr}H=$ the number of parameters — the trace-equals-rank fact above, used as a degrees-of-freedom count. Non-orthogonal (oblique) projections show up in oblique factor rotation and in some signal-separation methods, which is why the general definition is worth having.

Linear maps & rank–nullity

Definitions $T:V\to W$ is linear if $T(u+\lambda v)=T(u)+\lambda T(v)$. Then $\ker T=\{v:T(v)=0\}\subseteq V$ and $\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}\subseteq W$ are subspaces, and $\operatorname{rank}T=\dim\operatorname{im}T$.
Rank–nullity $$\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T .$$ What gets collapsed plus what survives accounts for the whole domain.
  • $T$ injective $\iff\ker T=\{0\}$. (For linear maps, checking one vector suffices — no need to compare pairs.)
  • $T$ surjective $\iff\operatorname{rank}T=\dim W$.
  • For $T:V\to V$ with $\dim V\lt\infty$: injective $\iff$ surjective $\iff$ bijective. This fails in infinite dimensions — the shift $(x_1,x_2,\dots)\mapsto(0,x_1,x_2,\dots)$ is injective but not surjective.
Sanity check for the projection section For a projection, rank–nullity is the direct-sum decomposition: $\dim V=\dim\ker p+\dim\operatorname{im}p$ matches $V=\operatorname{im}p\oplus\ker p$. The extra content of the projection theorem is that the two subspaces intersect trivially and together span — not merely that the dimensions add up.

Matrix representation & change of basis

Fix a basis $\mathcal{B}=(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ of $V$ and $\mathcal{C}$ of $W$. The matrix $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$ has as its $j$-th column the coordinates of $T(e_j)$ in $\mathcal{C}$. Composition becomes matrix multiplication.

Change of basis Let $P$ be the change-of-basis matrix (its columns are the new basis vectors written in the old basis). For an endomorphism $T:V\to V$, $$[T]_{\text{new}}=P^{-1}[T]_{\text{old}}P .$$ Two matrices related this way are similar — the same map seen from a different angle. For a map between different spaces it is $B^{-1}[T]A$, called equivalence.
Similar ≠ congruent Similarity $P^{-1}MP$ is for maps; congruence $P^{\mathsf T}MP$ (Chapter 3) is for bilinear forms. They preserve different things — similarity preserves eigenvalues, congruence preserves the signature. Using the wrong one is a standard slip.

The dual space

Definition $V^{*}=\{\varphi:V\to\mathbb{F}\ \text{linear}\}$, the linear functionals. Given a basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ of $V$, the dual basis $(\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_n)$ is defined by $$\varphi_i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}.$$ So $\dim V^{*}=\dim V$ in finite dimensions.

Concretely $\varphi_i$ reads off the $i$-th coordinate: $\varphi_i\big(\sum_j\lambda_je_j\big)=\lambda_i$. Every functional is $\varphi=\sum_i\varphi(e_i)\varphi_i$.

The isomorphism is not canonical $V\cong V^{*}$ holds, but the identification depends on the basis you chose — change the basis and the correspondence changes. This is why Chapter 2's Riesz theorem is worth stating separately: an inner product gives a canonical identification $\varphi\leftrightarrow u$ via $\varphi(v)=\langle v,u\rangle$, no basis required. Riesz is the upgrade of this section.

Metric & normed spaces, operator norm

Norm axioms (1) $\lVert v\rVert\ge0$, with $\lVert v\rVert=0$ only for $v=0$; (2) $\lVert\lambda v\rVert=|\lambda|\,\lVert v\rVert$; (3) $\lVert v+w\rVert\le\lVert v\rVert+\lVert w\rVert$. Every norm induces a metric $d(v,w)=\lVert v-w\rVert$.
NormFormulaUnit ball / use
$\lVert x\rVert_1$$\sum_i|x_i|$diamond · LASSO, induces sparsity
$\lVert x\rVert_2$$\sqrt{\sum_ix_i^{2}}$circle · least squares, ridge
$\lVert x\rVert_\infty$$\max_i|x_i|$square · worst-case error
Operator norm $$\lVert T\rVert=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{\lVert Tv\rVert}{\lVert v\rVert}=\sup_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\lVert Tv\rVert$$ — the worst stretch $T$ applies to any unit vector. It satisfies $\lVert Tv\rVert\le\lVert T\rVert\lVert v\rVert$ and is submultiplicative: $\lVert ST\rVert\le\lVert S\rVert\lVert T\rVert$.
In finite dimensions all norms are equivalent For any two norms there are constants $0\lt c\le C$ with $c\lVert v\rVert_a\le\lVert v\rVert_b\le C\lVert v\rVert_a$. So convergence, open sets and continuity do not depend on which norm you pick — a genuinely finite-dimensional luxury. The constants still matter numerically, and they can grow with the dimension.
Looking ahead For the Euclidean norm the operator norm turns out to be $\sigma_1$, the largest singular value — see the SVD. That is the bridge from this chapter's abstract definition to something you can actually compute.

Traps

TrapReality
"Projection means orthogonal projection"Not in Chapter 1. A projection is any $p$ with $p^{2}=p$; orthogonality is the extra condition $p^{*}=p$, and needs an inner product.
"Project onto $U$" is a complete instructionIt is not — you must also say along which complement. Complements are far from unique.
"$\operatorname{im}p$ and $\ker p$ might overlap"For a projection they never do: $v=p(u)$ and $p(v)=0$ force $v=p^{2}(u)=p(v)=0$.
"$s$ is a symmetry, so it is an isometry"No. $s^{2}=\operatorname{id}$ says nothing about lengths — example (a) above distorts them. Only orthogonal symmetries preserve length.
$U\cup W$ is a subspaceOnly if one contains the other. Use $U+W$.
Similar vs congruent$P^{-1}MP$ for maps, $P^{\mathsf T}MP$ for forms.
$V\cong V^{*}$ "naturally"The isomorphism depends on a basis. Riesz gives the canonical one, but only with an inner product.
Injective $\Rightarrow$ surjectiveTrue only for $T:V\to V$ with $\dim V$ finite.
