The eight theorems

Chapters 2, 5 and 6 rest on eight results, and they form a single chain: Gram–Schmidt builds orthonormal bases → projection gives least squares → Riesz gives adjoints → Schur triangularizes → the spectral theorem diagonalizes → the SVD extends that to every matrix. Learn them in this order and each one hands you the next.

The chain: ① Gram–Schmidt · ② Orthogonal projection & least squares · ③ Riesz representation · ④ Adjoints · ⑤ Schur · ⑥ Cayley–Hamilton · ⑦ Spectral theorem · ⑧ SVD · how they connect
On numbering The textbook numbers its results; the only number confirmed here is Theorem 6.9 = the SVD (§6.3, added in the 29 Jun version). Everything else is referenced by chapter and name rather than by a number I cannot check against your copy. If you are quoting a result in an exam, use the number printed in your PDF — names are safe, invented numbers are not.

①Gram–Schmidt CH 2

Theorem — Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization Let $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ be a linearly independent list in an inner-product space $V$. Then there is an orthonormal list $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ such that $$\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_k)=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_k)\qquad\text{for every }k=1,\dots,n.$$
Main idea Process the vectors one at a time. At each step, subtract off everything you already have — the projection onto the span built so far — and normalise what is left. Independence is exactly what guarantees the leftover is non-zero.

Construction Set $e_1=v_1/\lVert v_1\rVert$ (legal: $v_1\ne0$ by independence). Having built $e_1,\dots,e_{k-1}$, put $$w_k=v_k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\langle v_k,e_j\rangle\,e_j,\qquad e_k=\frac{w_k}{\lVert w_k\rVert}.$$

$w_k\ne0$ Suppose $w_k=0$. Then $$v_k=\sum_{j\lt k}\langle v_k,e_j\rangle e_j\in\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_{k-1})=\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}),$$ using the inductive hypothesis for the last equality — and that contradicts linear independence. So $w_k\ne0$ and the division is legal.

Orthogonality For $i\lt k$, using $\langle e_j,e_i\rangle=\delta_{ji}$: $$\langle w_k,e_i\rangle=\langle v_k,e_i\rangle-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\langle v_k,e_j\rangle\langle e_j,e_i\rangle=\langle v_k,e_i\rangle-\langle v_k,e_i\rangle=0 .$$ And $\lVert e_k\rVert=1$ by construction, so $(e_1,\dots,e_k)$ is orthonormal.

Spans agree $e_k$ is a combination of $v_k$ and $e_1,\dots,e_{k-1}$, so $e_k\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_k)$; conversely $v_k=\lVert w_k\rVert e_k+\sum_{j\lt k}\langle v_k,e_j\rangle e_j$ lies in $\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_k)$. Both spans have dimension $k$, so they are equal. ∎

Corollaries (a) Every finite-dimensional inner-product space has an orthonormal basis. (b) Every orthonormal list extends to an orthonormal basis. (c) QR decomposition: applying the process to the columns of $A$ (full column rank) gives $A=QR$ with $Q$ having orthonormal columns and $R$ upper triangular with positive diagonal.
Why data science cares Orthonormal bases make coordinates free: $v=\sum\langle v,e_j\rangle e_j$, no linear system to solve. QR is also the numerically stable way to solve least squares — forming $A^{*}A$ squares the condition number, which is why numpy.linalg.lstsq uses a factorization instead.

②Orthogonal projection & least squares CH 2

Theorem — best approximation Let $U\subseteq V$ be a finite-dimensional subspace with orthonormal basis $(e_1,\dots,e_m)$, and define $$P_Uv=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle v,e_j\rangle e_j .$$ Then (i) $v-P_Uv\perp U$, and (ii) for every $u\in U$, $$\lVert v-P_Uv\rVert\le\lVert v-u\rVert,$$ with equality only for $u=P_Uv$. So $P_Uv$ is the unique closest point of $U$ to $v$.
Main idea One line of Pythagoras. Split $v-u$ into the part perpendicular to $U$ and the part inside $U$; the perpendicular part is fixed, so you minimise by killing the inside part.

(i) The residual is orthogonal For each $i$, $$\langle v-P_Uv,\;e_i\rangle=\langle v,e_i\rangle-\sum_{j=1}^m\langle v,e_j\rangle\langle e_j,e_i\rangle=\langle v,e_i\rangle-\langle v,e_i\rangle=0,$$ and since the $e_i$ span $U$, $v-P_Uv\perp U$.

(ii) Minimality Let $u\in U$. Write $$v-u=\underbrace{(v-P_Uv)}_{\perp\,U}+\underbrace{(P_Uv-u)}_{\in\,U}.$$ The two pieces are orthogonal, so by the Pythagorean identity $$\lVert v-u\rVert^{2}=\lVert v-P_Uv\rVert^{2}+\lVert P_Uv-u\rVert^{2}\;\ge\;\lVert v-P_Uv\rVert^{2},$$ with equality iff $\lVert P_Uv-u\rVert=0$, i.e. $u=P_Uv$. ∎

Least squares and the normal equations

Given $A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}$ and $b\in\mathbb{C}^m$, the problem $\min_x\lVert Ax-b\rVert$ asks for the closest point to $b$ in $U=\operatorname{col}(A)$. By the theorem the minimiser is characterised by $b-Ax\perp\operatorname{col}(A)$, i.e. $A^{*}(b-Ax)=0$: $$\boxed{\,A^{*}Ax=A^{*}b\,}$$ If $A$ has full column rank then $A^{*}A$ is invertible, so $x=(A^{*}A)^{-1}A^{*}b$ and the projection matrix onto $\operatorname{col}(A)$ is $P=A(A^{*}A)^{-1}A^{*}$.

Two facts worth checking yourself $P^{2}=P$ (projecting twice changes nothing) and $P^{*}=P$ (orthogonal projections are self-adjoint). Together these two properties characterise orthogonal projections.
Why data science cares This is linear regression. Fitting $y\approx X\beta$ by least squares is projecting $y$ onto the column space of the design matrix; the residual vector is orthogonal to every feature, which is why residuals-vs-fitted plots should show no structure. Ridge regression adds $\lambda I$ to $A^{*}A$ precisely to keep it invertible when the features are collinear.

③Riesz representation CH 2

Theorem — Riesz representation Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner-product space and $\varphi:V\to\mathbb{F}$ a linear functional. Then there is a unique $u\in V$ with $$\varphi(v)=\langle v,u\rangle\quad\text{for all }v\in V.$$
Main idea Every linear functional is "take the inner product with something". Expand $v$ in an orthonormal basis, and the coefficients of $\varphi$ assemble into the representing vector.

Existence Pick an orthonormal basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ — this is where Gram–Schmidt is used. Every $v$ satisfies $v=\sum_j\langle v,e_j\rangle e_j$, so by linearity $$\varphi(v)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle v,e_j\rangle\,\varphi(e_j)=\Big\langle v,\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overline{\varphi(e_j)}\,e_j\Big\rangle .$$ So $u=\sum_j\overline{\varphi(e_j)}\,e_j$ works. (The conjugate appears because the inner product is conjugate-linear in its second slot.)

Uniqueness If $\langle v,u_1\rangle=\langle v,u_2\rangle$ for all $v$, then $\langle v,u_1-u_2\rangle=0$ for all $v$; take $v=u_1-u_2$ to get $\lVert u_1-u_2\rVert^{2}=0$, hence $u_1=u_2$. ∎

Finite dimension matters The proof uses a finite orthonormal basis. In infinite dimensions the statement still holds for continuous functionals on a complete space (a Hilbert space) — and both hypotheses are genuinely needed. Your course is finite-dimensional, so the short proof above is the one to know.

④Adjoint operators CH 2

Theorem — existence and uniqueness of the adjoint Let $T:V\to W$ be linear between finite-dimensional inner-product spaces. There is a unique linear map $T^{*}:W\to V$ with $$\langle Tv,\,w\rangle_W=\langle v,\,T^{*}w\rangle_V\qquad\text{for all }v\in V,\;w\in W.$$
Main idea Freeze $w$. Then $v\mapsto\langle Tv,w\rangle$ is a linear functional on $V$ — and Riesz says every such functional is an inner product with a unique vector. Call that vector $T^{*}w$. The adjoint is Riesz applied one $w$ at a time.

Existence Fix $w\in W$ and define $\varphi_w(v)=\langle Tv,w\rangle_W$. This is linear in $v$ (composition of $T$ with a linear functional), so by Riesz there is a unique vector — name it $T^{*}w$ — with $\varphi_w(v)=\langle v,T^{*}w\rangle_V$. That is the defining identity.

$T^{*}$ is linear For $w_1,w_2\in W$ and scalars, every $v$ gives $$\langle v,T^{*}(w_1+\lambda w_2)\rangle=\langle Tv,w_1+\lambda w_2\rangle=\langle Tv,w_1\rangle+\bar\lambda\langle Tv,w_2\rangle=\langle v,\;T^{*}w_1+\lambda T^{*}w_2\rangle .$$ Since this holds for all $v$, the uniqueness half of Riesz forces $T^{*}(w_1+\lambda w_2)=T^{*}w_1+\lambda T^{*}w_2$. Uniqueness of $T^{*}$ follows the same way. ∎

The properties to memorise
  • In orthonormal bases, the matrix of $T^{*}$ is the conjugate transpose of the matrix of $T$: $[T^{*}]=\overline{[T]}^{\,\mathsf T}=[T]^{*}$. (Orthonormality is essential — in a general basis this is false.)
  • $(S+T)^{*}=S^{*}+T^{*}$, $(\lambda T)^{*}=\bar\lambda T^{*}$, $(ST)^{*}=T^{*}S^{*}$ (order reverses), $(T^{*})^{*}=T$.
  • $\ker T^{*}=(\operatorname{im}T)^{\perp}$ and $\operatorname{im}T^{*}=(\ker T)^{\perp}$ — the four fundamental subspaces.

Vocabulary that the rest of the page needs: $T$ is self-adjoint (Hermitian) if $T=T^{*}$; unitary if $T^{*}T=I$; and normal if $TT^{*}=T^{*}T$. Self-adjoint and unitary operators are both normal — that is why the spectral theorem is stated for normal operators.

⑤Schur's lemma — unitary triangularization CH 6

Theorem — Schur Every $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ is unitarily similar to an upper triangular matrix: there is a unitary $U$ and upper triangular $T$ with $$A=UTU^{*}.$$ The diagonal of $T$ carries the eigenvalues of $A$, with multiplicity.
Main idea Induction on $n$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ every matrix has at least one eigenvector; make it the first vector of an orthonormal basis. In that basis the first column is $(\lambda_1,0,\dots,0)^{\mathsf T}$, so the matrix splits into a $1\times1$ block and an $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ block. Recurse on the small block.

Base case $n=1$: nothing to do, $A$ is already triangular.

Inductive step The characteristic polynomial $\chi_A$ has degree $n\ge1$, so over $\mathbb{C}$ — algebraically closed — it has a root $\lambda_1$. Hence there is an eigenvector, which we scale to a unit vector $u_1$. Extend $u_1$ to an orthonormal basis $(u_1,\dots,u_n)$ of $\mathbb{C}^n$ (Gram–Schmidt again) and let $U_1=[\,u_1\ \cdots\ u_n\,]$, which is unitary. Because $Au_1=\lambda_1u_1$, the first column of $U_1^{*}AU_1$ is $\lambda_1 e_1$: $$U_1^{*}AU_1=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&*\\[2pt]0&A'\end{pmatrix},\qquad A'\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)} .$$ By induction $A'=U'T'U'^{*}$ with $U'$ unitary and $T'$ upper triangular. Put $$U=U_1\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&U'\end{pmatrix},$$ a product of unitaries, hence unitary. Then $U^{*}AU$ is upper triangular. ∎

The diagonal Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, and for a triangular matrix $\chi_T(\lambda)=\prod_i(\lambda-t_{ii})$. So the diagonal entries of $T$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $A$.

Over $\mathbb{R}$ this fails A real rotation by $90^\circ$, $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, has no real eigenvalue, so it cannot be triangularized over $\mathbb{R}$. Schur is a complex theorem; the real analogue only gives block-triangular form with $1\times1$ and $2\times2$ blocks.

⑥Cayley–Hamilton CH 5

Theorem — Cayley–Hamilton Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial. With $\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)$, $$\chi_A(A)=0 .$$
The famous fake proof — do not write this "$\chi_A(A)=\det(AI-A)=\det(0)=0$." This is wrong. $\chi_A(\lambda)$ is a polynomial with scalar coefficients; substituting the matrix $A$ for $\lambda$ is not the same operation as substituting it inside the determinant, where $\lambda I-A$ would become a matrix of matrices. Examiners look for this.
Main idea (via Schur) Triangularize. For a triangular $T$ with diagonal $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$, the product $(T-\lambda_1I)\cdots(T-\lambda_kI)$ annihilates the first $k$ basis vectors — each factor kills one more. After $n$ factors it kills everything.

Proof over $\mathbb{C}$ By Schur, $A=UTU^{*}$ with $T$ upper triangular, diagonal $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Similar matrices share a characteristic polynomial, so $\chi_A=\chi_T=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda-\lambda_i)$.

Let $M_k=(T-\lambda_1I)(T-\lambda_2I)\cdots(T-\lambda_kI)$. Claim: $M_k e_j=0$ for all $j\le k$, where $e_j$ is the $j$-th standard basis vector.

  • $k=1$: $T$ is upper triangular with $t_{11}=\lambda_1$, so $Te_1=\lambda_1e_1$ and $(T-\lambda_1I)e_1=0$.
  • Step: assume $M_{k-1}e_j=0$ for $j\le k-1$. Since $T$ is upper triangular, $(T-\lambda_kI)e_k$ is a combination of $e_1,\dots,e_{k-1}$ (the $e_k$ component is $t_{kk}-\lambda_k=0$). Applying $M_{k-1}$ — which commutes with the factors, all being polynomials in $T$ — kills it. So $M_ke_k=M_{k-1}(T-\lambda_kI)e_k=0$, and $M_ke_j=0$ for $j\lt k$ as well because $M_k=M_{k-1}(T-\lambda_kI)$ and the factors commute.

Hence $M_n$ kills every basis vector, i.e. $\chi_T(T)=M_n=0$. Finally $$\chi_A(A)=\chi_T(UTU^{*})=U\,\chi_T(T)\,U^{*}=0,$$ using that $(UTU^{*})^{k}=UT^{k}U^{*}$, so any polynomial applied to $A$ conjugates the same way. ∎

What it buys you $A^{n}$ is a combination of $I,A,\dots,A^{n-1}$, so every power — and every matrix function defined by a power series, such as $e^{tA}$ — collapses into that finite span. For invertible $A$ it also gives $A^{-1}$ as a polynomial in $A$: rearrange $\chi_A(A)=0$ and divide by the constant term $\pm\det A$.

⑦The spectral theorem CH 6

Theorem — spectral theorem for normal operators For $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent: (i) $A$ is normal ($AA^{*}=A^{*}A$); (ii) $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $A$; (iii) $A=UDU^{*}$ with $U$ unitary and $D$ diagonal.
Main idea Schur already gives $A=UTU^{*}$ with $T$ triangular. Normality survives unitary conjugation, so $T$ is a normal triangular matrix — and a short computation forces such a matrix to be diagonal. The whole theorem is "Schur plus one calculation".

(iii) ⇒ (ii) Immediate: the columns of $U$ are orthonormal and $AU=UD$ says exactly that column $j$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $d_{jj}$.

(ii) ⇒ (iii) Put the orthonormal eigenbasis in the columns of $U$; then $U$ is unitary and $U^{*}AU=D$.

(iii) ⇒ (i) $AA^{*}=UDU^{*}UD^{*}U^{*}=UDD^{*}U^{*}$ and likewise $A^{*}A=UD^{*}DU^{*}$. Diagonal matrices commute, so the two agree.

(i) ⇒ (iii) — the real content By Schur, $A=UTU^{*}$ with $T$ upper triangular. Then $$TT^{*}=U^{*}AUU^{*}A^{*}U=U^{*}AA^{*}U=U^{*}A^{*}AU=T^{*}T,$$ so $T$ is normal. Now compare the $(1,1)$ entries. Since $T$ is upper triangular, $$(TT^{*})_{11}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|t_{1j}|^{2},\qquad (T^{*}T)_{11}=|t_{11}|^{2}.$$ Equality forces $\sum_{j\ge2}|t_{1j}|^{2}=0$, hence $t_{1j}=0$ for all $j\ge2$: the first row is zero off the diagonal. So $T$ splits as $\begin{pmatrix}t_{11}&0\\0&T''\end{pmatrix}$ with $T''$ upper triangular and (again) normal. Induct on the size to conclude $T$ is diagonal. ∎

Why data science cares A covariance matrix is real symmetric and positive semidefinite, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis with non-negative eigenvalues. That is PCA: principal components are the eigenvectors, explained variance is the eigenvalues. Spectral clustering and PageRank are the same theorem wearing different hats.

⑧Singular Value Decomposition CH 6 · THM 6.9

Theorem 6.9 — SVD Every $A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}$ factors as $$A=U\Sigma V^{*}$$ with $U\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}$ unitary, $V\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ unitary, and $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ zero off the main diagonal with entries $$\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge\sigma_r\gt0=\sigma_{r+1}=\dots,\qquad r=\operatorname{rank}A .$$ The $\sigma_i$ are the singular values, and $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^{*}A)}$.
Main idea $A$ itself may not be diagonalizable — it need not even be square. But $A^{*}A$ is Hermitian and positive semidefinite, so the spectral theorem applies to it. Diagonalize $A^{*}A$, take square roots of the eigenvalues, and push the eigenbasis through $A$ to get the second orthonormal family.

Step 1 — apply the spectral theorem to $A^{*}A$ $(A^{*}A)^{*}=A^{*}A$, so it is Hermitian; and for any $x$, $$\langle A^{*}Ax,x\rangle=\langle Ax,Ax\rangle=\lVert Ax\rVert^{2}\ge0,$$ so it is positive semidefinite and its eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ are real and $\ge0$. By ⑦ there is an orthonormal basis $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ of $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ with $A^{*}Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$. Order so that $\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_n\ge0$ and set $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Let $r$ be the number of non-zero $\sigma_i$.

Step 2 — build the left family For $i\le r$ define $$u_i=\frac{1}{\sigma_i}Av_i .$$ These are orthonormal: $$\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\frac{1}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\langle Av_i,Av_j\rangle=\frac{1}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\langle A^{*}Av_i,v_j\rangle=\frac{\lambda_i}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_j}\delta_{ij}=\delta_{ij}.$$ Extend $(u_1,\dots,u_r)$ to an orthonormal basis $(u_1,\dots,u_m)$ of $\mathbb{C}^{m}$ (Gram–Schmidt).

Step 3 — the tail vanishes For $i\gt r$ we have $\lambda_i=0$, so $$\lVert Av_i\rVert^{2}=\langle A^{*}Av_i,v_i\rangle=\lambda_i\lVert v_i\rVert^{2}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;Av_i=0 .$$

Step 4 — assemble We now have $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$ for $i\le r$ and $Av_i=0$ for $i\gt r$ — which is exactly $AV=U\Sigma$ read column by column. Since $V$ is unitary, $A=U\Sigma V^{*}$. ∎

Also $\operatorname{rank}A=r$: the $v_i$ with $i\gt r$ span $\ker A$, and the $u_i$ with $i\le r$ are an orthonormal basis of $\operatorname{im}A$.

What the SVD hands you
  • Rank $=$ number of non-zero singular values — and the numerically honest notion of rank (count the $\sigma_i$ above a tolerance).
  • Operator norm $\lVert A\rVert_2=\sigma_1$; Frobenius norm $\lVert A\rVert_F=\sqrt{\sum_i\sigma_i^{2}}$.
  • Condition number $\kappa_2(A)=\sigma_1/\sigma_r$ — how much the map stretches versus squashes.
  • Pseudoinverse $A^{+}=V\Sigma^{+}U^{*}$, where $\Sigma^{+}$ inverts the non-zero singular values and transposes. $x=A^{+}b$ is the minimum-norm least-squares solution — ② without the full-rank assumption.
  • Eckart–Young: truncating to the largest $k$ singular values, $A_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{*}$, is the best rank-$k$ approximation in both norms above.
Why data science cares The SVD is PCA without needing to form a covariance matrix (numerically better), it is the engine behind latent semantic analysis and recommender systems, and Eckart–Young is the theorem that makes "keep the top $k$ components" a provably optimal compression rather than a heuristic.
Mini-lab — do this once by hand Take $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{pmatrix}$. Compute $A^{\mathsf T}A$, its eigenvalues and eigenvectors, then $\sigma_i$, then $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$. Verify against: 
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[3.,0.],[4.,5.]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
print(s)                      # your sigmas, descending
print(np.allclose(A, U @ np.diag(s) @ Vt))   # True
Signs of $u_i,v_i$ may differ from yours — the SVD is unique only up to those choices when singular values are distinct, and less unique still when they repeat.
