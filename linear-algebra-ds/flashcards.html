What must you check to prove that $U\subseteq V$ is a subspace?
Three things: (1) $0\in U$ (so $U\ne\emptyset$); (2) closed under addition, $u,w\in U\Rightarrow u+w\in U$; (3) closed under scalar multiplication, $\lambda\in\mathbb{F},u\in U\Rightarrow\lambda u\in U$. Checking $0\in U$ is the cheap way to disprove — e.g. $\{x:\sum x_i=1\}$ fails immediately.
Define linearly independent, spanning, basis and dimension.
Independent: $\sum\lambda_iv_i=0\Rightarrow$ all $\lambda_i=0$. Spanning: every $v\in V$ is some $\sum\lambda_iv_i$. Basis: both at once — equivalently, every $v$ has a unique representation. Dimension: the common size of every basis (well-defined by the exchange lemma).
When is a sum of subspaces a direct sum $U\oplus W$, and what is the dimension formula?
$V=U\oplus W$ iff $V=U+W$ and $U\cap W=\{0\}$ — equivalently, every $v$ writes uniquely as $u+w$. In general $\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)$, so for a direct sum $\dim(U\oplus W)=\dim U+\dim W$.
State the rank–nullity theorem and say what it means geometrically.
For linear $T:V\to W$ with $\dim V\lt\infty$: $$\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T.$$ Whatever $T$ collapses (the kernel) plus whatever survives (the image) accounts for the whole domain. Consequence: for $T:V\to V$, injective $\iff$ surjective $\iff$ bijective.
What is the dual space $V^{*}$, and what is the dual basis?
$V^{*}=\{\varphi:V\to\mathbb{F}\ \text{linear}\}$, the space of linear functionals. If $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ is a basis of $V$, the dual basis $(\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_n)$ is defined by $\varphi_i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}$. So $\dim V^{*}=\dim V$ — but the isomorphism is basis-dependent, which is why the inner-product version (Riesz) is the useful one.
How is the matrix of a linear map built, and how does it change under a change of basis?
Column $j$ of $[T]$ holds the coordinates of $T(e_j)$ in the target basis. Change of basis: $[T]_{\text{new}}=P^{-1}[T]_{\text{old}}P$ where $P$ is the change-of-basis matrix — matrices of the same map are similar. For a map between different spaces it is $B^{-1}[T]A$ (equivalence).
Give the norm axioms and the definition of the operator norm.
Norm: (1) $\lVert v\rVert\ge0$ with equality iff $v=0$; (2) $\lVert\lambda v\rVert=|\lambda|\lVert v\rVert$; (3) triangle inequality $\lVert v+w\rVert\le\lVert v\rVert+\lVert w\rVert$. Operator norm: $$\lVert T\rVert=\sup_{v\ne0}\frac{\lVert Tv\rVert}{\lVert v\rVert}=\sup_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\lVert Tv\rVert$$ — the largest stretch factor. It is submultiplicative: $\lVert ST\rVert\le\lVert S\rVert\lVert T\rVert$.
What characterises a projection, and what extra condition makes it an orthogonal projection?
A projection is a linear $P$ with $P^{2}=P$ (idempotent); then $V=\operatorname{im}P\oplus\ker P$. It is an orthogonal projection iff additionally $P^{*}=P$ (self-adjoint) — that is exactly what forces the two summands to be orthogonal rather than merely complementary.
List the inner-product axioms over $\mathbb{C}$.
(1) Linear in the first slot: $\langle\alpha u+v,w\rangle=\alpha\langle u,w\rangle+\langle v,w\rangle$. (2) Conjugate symmetry: $\langle v,w\rangle=\overline{\langle w,v\rangle}$ — hence conjugate-linear in the second slot. (3) Positive definite: $\langle v,v\rangle\ge0$, $=0$ only for $v=0$. Then $\lVert v\rVert=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$.
State Cauchy–Schwarz, including the equality case.
$$|\langle u,v\rangle|\le\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert,$$ with equality iff $u$ and $v$ are linearly dependent. Proof idea: expand $\lVert u-\tfrac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\lVert v\rVert^{2}}v\rVert^{2}\ge0$ — i.e. subtract the projection and use positivity. It is what makes the triangle inequality work.
Why is an orthonormal basis so much better than an ordinary one?
Coordinates become free: $v=\sum_j\langle v,e_j\rangle e_j$ — no linear system to solve. Also $\lVert v\rVert^{2}=\sum_j|\langle v,e_j\rangle|^{2}$ (Parseval), and the change-of-basis matrix between orthonormal bases is unitary, so its inverse is its conjugate transpose.
Write the Gram–Schmidt step, and say where linear independence is used.
$$w_k=v_k-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\langle v_k,e_j\rangle e_j,\qquad e_k=\frac{w_k}{\lVert w_k\rVert}.$$ Independence guarantees $w_k\ne0$ — otherwise $v_k$ would lie in $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{k-1})$ and you would be dividing by zero. Spans are preserved at every stage; the matrix version is QR.
Give the orthogonal projection formula and the property that makes it optimal.
$P_Uv=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle v,e_j\rangle e_j$ for an orthonormal basis of $U$. Then $v-P_Uv\perp U$, and for all $u\in U$ $$\lVert v-u\rVert^{2}=\lVert v-P_Uv\rVert^{2}+\lVert P_Uv-u\rVert^{2}\ge\lVert v-P_Uv\rVert^{2}$$ by Pythagoras — so $P_Uv$ is the unique closest point.
Derive the normal equations for $\min_x\lVert Ax-b\rVert$.
The minimiser is the projection of $b$ onto $\operatorname{col}(A)$, characterised by $b-Ax\perp\operatorname{col}(A)$, i.e. $A^{*}(b-Ax)=0$: $$A^{*}Ax=A^{*}b.$$ Full column rank $\Rightarrow x=(A^{*}A)^{-1}A^{*}b$ and $P=A(A^{*}A)^{-1}A^{*}$. In practice use QR or the SVD — forming $A^{*}A$ squares the condition number.
State the Riesz representation theorem and give the representing vector.
Every linear functional $\varphi$ on a finite-dimensional inner-product space is $\varphi(v)=\langle v,u\rangle$ for a unique $u$. Explicitly $u=\sum_j\overline{\varphi(e_j)}\,e_j$ in any orthonormal basis. Uniqueness: if $\langle v,u_1-u_2\rangle=0$ for all $v$, take $v=u_1-u_2$.
Define the adjoint $T^{*}$, say why it exists, and give its matrix.
Defined by $\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^{*}w\rangle$ for all $v,w$. Existence: fix $w$; then $v\mapsto\langle Tv,w\rangle$ is a functional, so Riesz supplies a unique vector $T^{*}w$. In orthonormal bases $[T^{*}]=\overline{[T]}^{\mathsf T}$ — the conjugate transpose. (False in a non-orthonormal basis.)
Give the two orthogonality relations between $T$ and $T^{*}$.
$$\ker T^{*}=(\operatorname{im}T)^{\perp},\qquad \operatorname{im}T^{*}=(\ker T)^{\perp}.$$ These are the four fundamental subspaces. They are why the least-squares residual is orthogonal to every column of $A$, and why $\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}A^{*}$.
Define self-adjoint, unitary and normal, and give the containment.
Self-adjoint: $T=T^{*}$. Unitary: $T^{*}T=TT^{*}=I$ (preserves inner products, hence lengths and angles). Normal: $TT^{*}=T^{*}T$. Both self-adjoint and unitary operators are normal — which is exactly the hypothesis of the spectral theorem, so it covers both at once.
Distinguish a bilinear from a sesquilinear form.
Bilinear: linear in both arguments — the natural notion over $\mathbb{R}$. Sesquilinear ("one and a half"): linear in one argument, conjugate-linear in the other — the right notion over $\mathbb{C}$. A complex inner product is a sesquilinear form that is Hermitian and positive definite.
How is a bilinear form represented by a matrix, and how does that matrix transform?
$B(u,v)=u^{\mathsf T}Mv$ with $M_{ij}=B(e_i,e_j)$. Under a change of basis $u=Px$: $$M\mapsto P^{\mathsf T}MP\quad(\text{or }P^{*}MP\text{ in the sesquilinear case}).$$ This is congruence, not similarity $P^{-1}MP$ — a standard confusion. Congruence preserves the signature, not the eigenvalues.
What is a quadratic form, and what does positive definite mean?
$q(v)=B(v,v)$ for a symmetric/Hermitian $B$; equivalently $q(x)=x^{*}Mx$ with $M=M^{*}$. It is positive definite if $q(v)\gt0$ for all $v\ne0$; positive semidefinite if $\ge0$. Equivalent tests: all eigenvalues $\gt0$; all leading principal minors $\gt0$ (Sylvester); $M=A^{*}A$ with $A$ of full column rank.
What is the defining property of the tensor product, and its dimension?
$V\otimes W$ linearises bilinear maps: every bilinear $B:V\times W\to Z$ factors uniquely through a linear map on $V\otimes W$ (universal property). $\dim(V\otimes W)=\dim V\cdot\dim W$, with basis $\{e_i\otimes f_j\}$. Contrast $\dim(V\oplus W)=\dim V+\dim W$ — sum adds, tensor multiplies.
Characterise the determinant by its defining properties.
The unique map $\det:(\mathbb{F}^{n})^{n}\to\mathbb{F}$ on the columns that is (1) $n$-linear (linear in each column separately), (2) alternating (zero whenever two columns are equal), (3) normalised, $\det I=1$. Everything else — expansion, sign rules, multiplicativity — follows from these three.
List the determinant properties you use constantly.
$\det(AB)=\det A\det B$; $\det A^{\mathsf T}=\det A$; $\det A^{*}=\overline{\det A}$; $\det(\lambda A)=\lambda^{n}\det A$; triangular $\Rightarrow$ product of the diagonal; $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A$. Row ops: swapping negates, scaling by $\lambda$ multiplies by $\lambda$, adding a multiple of another row changes nothing.
Complete: $A$ is invertible $\iff\dots$ (give five equivalents).
$\det A\ne0$ $\iff$ $\ker A=\{0\}$ $\iff$ columns are linearly independent $\iff$ $\operatorname{rank}A=n$ $\iff$ $0$ is not an eigenvalue $\iff$ all singular values are $\gt0$. (For square $A$ only.)
What does $|\det A|$ mean geometrically, and what does the sign mean?
$|\det A|$ is the volume scaling factor: the unit cube maps to a parallelepiped of volume $|\det A|$. The sign records orientation — negative means the map reverses it (a reflection). $\det A=0$ means the image is squashed into a lower-dimensional set, i.e. zero volume.
State Cramer's rule and say why nobody computes with it.
For invertible $A$, the solution of $Ax=b$ has $$x_i=\frac{\det A_i}{\det A},$$ where $A_i$ is $A$ with column $i$ replaced by $b$. It is a theoretical tool (it shows the solution depends smoothly on the data); computationally it needs $n+1$ determinants and is hopeless next to Gaussian elimination's $O(n^{3})$.
Define eigenvalue, eigenvector and eigenspace.
$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ if $Tv=\lambda v$ for some $v\ne0$; such a $v$ is an eigenvector. The eigenspace is $E_\lambda=\ker(T-\lambda I)$ — a subspace, containing $0$ together with all eigenvectors for $\lambda$. Note $v\ne0$ is part of the definition; $0$ is never an eigenvector.
What is the characteristic polynomial, and what do its coefficients tell you?
$\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)$, monic of degree $n$; its roots are exactly the eigenvalues. Two coefficients are free information: $$\sum_i\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}A,\qquad \prod_i\lambda_i=\det A.$$ Both are excellent arithmetic checks after computing eigenvalues.
Distinguish algebraic and geometric multiplicity, and give the inequality.
Algebraic $m_a(\lambda)$: multiplicity as a root of $\chi_A$. Geometric $m_g(\lambda)=\dim E_\lambda$: how many independent eigenvectors. Always $$1\le m_g(\lambda)\le m_a(\lambda).$$ A strict inequality anywhere means the matrix is defective — not diagonalizable. Example: $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ has $m_a=2$, $m_g=1$.
Give the criteria for diagonalizability.
$A$ is diagonalizable $\iff$ there is a basis of eigenvectors $\iff$ $\sum_\lambda m_g(\lambda)=n$ $\iff$ $m_g(\lambda)=m_a(\lambda)$ for every eigenvalue. Sufficient (not necessary): $n$ distinct eigenvalues. Then $A=PDP^{-1}$ with eigenvectors in the columns of $P$.
Give the diagonalization workflow, start to finish.
1. $\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)$. 2. Find the roots $\to$ eigenvalues with $m_a$. 3. For each, solve $(A-\lambda I)v=0$ for a basis of $E_\lambda$ $\to$ $m_g$. 4. If $\sum m_g=n$, stack the eigenvectors into $P$ and the eigenvalues into $D$; then $A=PDP^{-1}$. 5. Check with $\operatorname{tr}$ and $\det$.
State Cayley–Hamilton — and name the classic fake proof.
$\chi_A(A)=0$: every matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial. The fake proof: "$\chi_A(A)=\det(AI-A)=0$" — wrong, because substituting a matrix into the polynomial is not the same as substituting it inside the determinant. Correct route: triangularize by Schur, then show $\prod_i(T-\lambda_iI)$ kills each basis vector in turn.
How do you compute $f(A)$ — say $e^{tA}$ — for diagonalizable $A$?
If $A=PDP^{-1}$ then $A^{k}=PD^{k}P^{-1}$, so for any power series $$f(A)=P\,f(D)\,P^{-1}=P\operatorname{diag}\big(f(\lambda_1),\dots,f(\lambda_n)\big)P^{-1}.$$ So $e^{tA}=P\operatorname{diag}(e^{t\lambda_i})P^{-1}$ — the solution operator of $\dot x=Ax$. Cayley–Hamilton also caps everything at degree $n-1$.
Which quantities are invariant under similarity $A\mapsto P^{-1}AP$?
Characteristic polynomial, eigenvalues with both multiplicities, trace, determinant, rank, and Jordan form. Not invariant: the eigenvectors (they transform by $P^{-1}$), the entries, and the singular values in general. Similarity = same map in a different basis.
Over $\mathbb{C}$, what can you always say about triangularization — and why does $\mathbb{R}$ fail?
Over $\mathbb{C}$ every matrix is similar (indeed unitarily similar, by Schur) to an upper triangular matrix, because $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed so $\chi_A$ always has a root. Over $\mathbb{R}$ this fails: $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has $\chi(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}+1$ with no real root.
State Schur's lemma and give the induction in one sentence.
Every $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ is unitarily similar to upper triangular: $A=UTU^{*}$, with the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $T$. Induction: over $\mathbb{C}$ take an eigenvalue and a unit eigenvector, extend it to an orthonormal basis (Gram–Schmidt), which makes the first column $\lambda_1e_1$; recurse on the trailing $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ block.
State the spectral theorem for normal operators, three ways.
For $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ these are equivalent: (i) $A$ is normal, $AA^{*}=A^{*}A$; (ii) $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $A$; (iii) $A=UDU^{*}$ with $U$ unitary, $D$ diagonal. Note the upgrade over plain diagonalizability: the eigenvector basis is orthonormal.
In the proof of the spectral theorem, why is a normal upper-triangular matrix diagonal?
Compare $(1,1)$ entries. For upper triangular $T$: $(TT^{*})_{11}=\sum_{j}|t_{1j}|^{2}$ but $(T^{*}T)_{11}=|t_{11}|^{2}$. Normality forces $\sum_{j\ge2}|t_{1j}|^{2}=0$, so the whole first row off the diagonal vanishes. Then induct on the trailing block. This one calculation is the entire content — the rest is Schur.
Prove in one line that a Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues.
Let $Av=\lambda v$ with $\lVert v\rVert=1$. Then $$\lambda=\langle Av,v\rangle=\langle v,Av\rangle=\overline{\langle Av,v\rangle}=\bar\lambda,$$ using $A=A^{*}$ and conjugate symmetry. So $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$. (Similarly: unitary $\Rightarrow|\lambda|=1$.)
What does the spectral theorem say for a real symmetric matrix?
$A=A^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is orthogonally diagonalizable: $A=QDQ^{\mathsf T}$ with $Q$ orthogonal ($Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I$) and $D$ real diagonal. No complex numbers are needed anywhere. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal; within a repeated eigenvalue you orthonormalise by Gram–Schmidt.
Give three equivalent characterisations of positive semidefinite for Hermitian $M$.
(1) $\langle Mx,x\rangle\ge0$ for all $x$; (2) all eigenvalues $\ge0$; (3) $M=A^{*}A$ for some $A$ (equivalently $M$ has a PSD square root). Positive definite replaces every $\ge$ by $\gt$. $A^{*}A$ is always PSD because $\langle A^{*}Ax,x\rangle=\lVert Ax\rVert^{2}$ — which is what launches the SVD.
State the SVD (Theorem 6.9). What are the shapes?
Every $A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}$ factors as $$A=U\Sigma V^{*}$$ with $U$ ($m\times m$) unitary, $V$ ($n\times n$) unitary, $\Sigma$ ($m\times n$) zero off the diagonal with $\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_r\gt0$, $r=\operatorname{rank}A$. No hypotheses: it works for every matrix, square or not, diagonalizable or not.
How is the SVD built? Where do the $v_i$, $\sigma_i$ and $u_i$ come from?
$A^{*}A$ is Hermitian PSD, so the spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis $v_1,\dots,v_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i\ge0$. Set $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ (descending) and, for $\sigma_i\gt0$, $$u_i=\frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i}.$$ These $u_i$ are orthonormal because $\langle Av_i,Av_j\rangle=\langle A^{*}Av_i,v_j\rangle=\lambda_i\delta_{ij}$; extend to a basis of $\mathbb{C}^{m}$. Then $AV=U\Sigma$.
How do singular values differ from eigenvalues?
Singular values exist for every matrix (any shape), are always real and $\ge0$, and are $\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^{*}A)}$. Eigenvalues need a square matrix, can be complex or negative, and may not come with enough eigenvectors. They coincide in absolute value only when $A$ is normal: then $\sigma_i=|\lambda_i|$.
Read off from the SVD: operator norm, Frobenius norm, rank, condition number.
$\lVert A\rVert_2=\sigma_1$ (largest stretch); $\lVert A\rVert_F=\sqrt{\sum_i\sigma_i^{2}}$; $\operatorname{rank}A=$ number of non-zero $\sigma_i$; $\kappa_2(A)=\sigma_1/\sigma_r$. The last one is why near-singular systems are dangerous: a large $\kappa$ means small data errors get amplified by roughly that factor.
State Eckart–Young. Why does it matter for data science?
Truncating the SVD, $A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{*}$, is the best rank-$k$ approximation of $A$ in both the operator and the Frobenius norm, with error $\sigma_{k+1}$ (resp. $\sqrt{\sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i^{2}}$). It turns "keep the top $k$ components" from a heuristic into a provably optimal compression — PCA, LSA, recommender systems.
Define the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse via the SVD and say what $A^{+}b$ solves.
$A^{+}=V\Sigma^{+}U^{*}$, where $\Sigma^{+}$ transposes $\Sigma$ and inverts the non-zero singular values. Then $x=A^{+}b$ is the minimum-norm least-squares solution: among all $x$ minimising $\lVert Ax-b\rVert$, it is the one with smallest $\lVert x\rVert$. This is least squares without assuming full column rank.
Explain PCA in the language of this course.
Centre the data matrix $X$. The covariance $\tfrac{1}{n-1}X^{\mathsf T}X$ is real symmetric PSD, so by the spectral theorem it has an orthonormal eigenbasis: those eigenvectors are the principal components and the eigenvalues are the explained variances. Equivalently, take the SVD $X=U\Sigma V^{*}$ — the columns of $V$ are the components and $\sigma_i^{2}/(n-1)$ the variances. The SVD route is preferred: it never forms $X^{\mathsf T}X$, so it does not square the condition number.
