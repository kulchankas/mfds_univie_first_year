Recurrence drills

Exam question 2 is always: "give asymptotically tight upper and lower bounds" — i.e. a justified $\Theta$. All four recurrences on the first-date paper were the case-2 / log-factor family, including one the vanilla Master Theorem can't solve. This page: the decision procedure, the polynomial growth condition Akra–Bazzi depends on, then 29 drills — the professor's own sheet-2 problems among them.

The decision procedure

For $T(n)=a\,T(n/b)+f(n)$ with $a\ge1$, $b>1$: compute the watershed $W(n)=n^{\log_b a}$ and compare $f$ against it.

SituationCaseAnswer
$f(n)=O\big(n^{\log_b a-\varepsilon}\big)$ — polynomially smaller1$T=\Theta\big(n^{\log_b a}\big)$ (leaves dominate)
$f(n)=\Theta\big(n^{\log_b a}\lg^k n\big)$, $k>-1$2 (extended)$T=\Theta\big(n^{\log_b a}\lg^{k+1} n\big)$ (all levels equal)
$f(n)=\Theta\big(n^{\log_b a}\lg^{-1} n\big)$  ($k=-1$)2*$T=\Theta\big(n^{\log_b a}\lg\lg n\big)$
$f(n)=\Theta\big(n^{\log_b a}\lg^{k} n\big)$, $k<-1$2*$T=\Theta\big(n^{\log_b a}\big)$ (the level sum converges)
$f(n)=\Omega\big(n^{\log_b a+\varepsilon}\big)$ — polynomially larger, and regularity $a f(n/b)\le c f(n)$, $c<1$3$T=\Theta\big(f(n)\big)$ (root dominates)
  • Polynomially smaller/larger means by a factor $n^{\varepsilon}$ — a $\lg$ factor is not enough for cases 1/3. That's exactly when the $k$-rows apply.
  • Claiming case 3? Check regularity explicitly: for $f(n)=n^s\lg^t n$ with $s>\log_b a$ it always holds ($af(n/b)\approx (a/b^s)f(n)$, $a/b^s<1$) — but write the one line.
  • Nothing fits (unequal splits, $T(n-1)$, $T(\sqrt n)$)? → Akra–Bazzi, direct expansion, or substitution — see below.
Akra–Bazzi in one box (full statement → proofs page) For $T(x)=g(x)+\sum_i a_iT(b_ix)$: solve $\;\sum_i a_ib_i^{\,p}=1\;$ for $p$, then $T(x)=\Theta\!\Big(x^p\big(1+\int_{1}^{x} g(u)\,u^{-p-1}\,du\big)\Big)$. Standard integrals: $\int\!\frac{du}{u}=\ln u$, $\;\int\!\frac{du}{u\ln u}=\ln\ln u$, $\;\int u^{s}\,du=\frac{u^{s+1}}{s+1}$ ($s\neq-1$).

The polynomial growth condition

Akra–Bazzi does not apply to an arbitrary driving function $g$ — it needs $g$ to satisfy the polynomial growth condition. Sheet 2 devotes four problems (22–25) to it, and opens with an erratum: the lecture book states it with the quantifiers in the wrong order. Use this form.

Definition — polynomial growth (corrected order) $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfies the polynomial growth condition if $$\forall \varphi>1\ \ \exists d>1\ \ \exists N>0\ \ \forall n\ge N\ \ \forall 1\le\psi\le\varphi:\qquad \tfrac{1}{d}\,f(n)\ \le\ f(\psi n)\ \le\ d\,f(n).$$ The $\exists N$ comes after the $\forall\varphi$ — i.e. $d$ and $N$ may both depend on $\varphi$. In words: rescaling the argument by any bounded factor changes the value by at most a constant factor.

Equivalent formulation (problem 23): for all $0<\varphi<1$ there are $N>0$ and $c_1,c_2>0$ with $c_1 f(n)\le f(u)\le c_2 f(n)$ for all $n\ge N$ and all $u\in[\varphi n,\,n]$. Same idea, stated on an interval instead of a scaling factor.

P1 Show $f(n)=n^2$ satisfies it, but $g(n)=2^n$ does not. (problem 22)

$n^2$ does. Given $\varphi>1$, take $d=\varphi^2$ and any $N>0$. For $1\le\psi\le\varphi$: $f(\psi n)=\psi^2n^2$, and $1\le\psi^2\le\varphi^2=d$, so $\frac1d f(n)\le f(\psi n)\le d f(n)$. ✓

$2^n$ does not. Fix $\varphi=2$ and take $\psi=2$. Then $g(\psi n)/g(n)=2^{2n}/2^{n}=2^{n}\to\infty$, so no constant $d$ can bound it for all $n\ge N$. ✗

The moral: polynomial and polylog functions pass; genuine exponentials fail. That is exactly why Akra–Bazzi is stated for the former.

P2 Show $f(n)=n\lg n$ and $f(n)=n\lg\lg n$ satisfy it. (problem 24)

$n\lg n$: for $1\le\psi\le\varphi$ and large $n$, $$\frac{f(\psi n)}{f(n)}=\psi\cdot\frac{\lg n+\lg\psi}{\lg n}=\psi\Big(1+\frac{\lg\psi}{\lg n}\Big).$$ This lies between $1$ and $\varphi(1+\lg\varphi)$ once $\lg n\ge 1$, so $d=\varphi(1+\lg\varphi)$ and $N=2$ work.

$n\lg\lg n$: identical, with $\lg\lg(\psi n)=\lg(\lg n+\lg\psi)\le\lg(2\lg n)=1+\lg\lg n$ for $n$ large. The ratio is again bounded by a constant depending only on $\varphi$.

Pattern: a $\lg$ inside shifts by an additive constant under scaling, which becomes a multiplicative factor $\to1$. Any $n^a\lg^b n$ passes.

P3 If $f$ has polynomial growth and $g(n)=\Theta(n)$, show $f(g(n))=\Theta(f(n))$ — and why "$f(\Theta(n))=\Theta(f(n))$" is legitimate notation. (problem 25)

$g(n)=\Theta(n)$ means $c_1n\le g(n)\le c_2n$ for $n\ge N_0$. Put $\varphi=\max(c_2,1/c_1)$ and let $d,N$ be as supplied by the polynomial growth condition for that $\varphi$. Writing $g(n)=\psi_n n$ with $\psi_n\in[c_1,c_2]$, the condition gives $\frac1d f(n)\le f(\psi_n n)\le d f(n)$ for $n$ large — i.e. $f(g(n))=\Theta(f(n))$.

Why the notation is fine: the conclusion depends only on $g$ lying in the class $\Theta(n)$, not on which member it is. So "$f(\Theta(n))$" names a well-defined $\Theta$-class, and the identity holds for every representative. This is what licenses the usual sloppiness of writing $T(n/2+O(1))=T(\Theta(n))$ inside recurrence manipulations — legitimate precisely when $f$ has polynomial growth.

Exam angle Two things are worth having ready: the corrected quantifier order (the professor flagged it himself, so it is fair game), and a one-line reason why $2^n$ fails. If asked to apply Akra–Bazzi rigorously, state that the driving function has polynomial growth before you use the theorem.
The other two tools Recursion tree — draw levels, sum level costs; how the Master Theorem is proved (Lemmas 4.2/4.3) and the honest fallback for anything odd. Substitution — guess the bound, prove it by induction with explicit constants; the rigorous way to verify a tight bound (used in Thm 9.2).
Exam habit Every answer = three visible steps: ① $a$, $b$, $W(n)=n^{\log_b a}$ computed; ② the comparison ("exact match with $k=1$", "polynomially smaller"); ③ the case named + the $\Theta$ answer. For case 3 add the regularity line. Two points each on the real paper — don't donate them to speed.

Drill bank

Groups A→D get harder. Do them cold, then check. EXAM 1 marks the actual first-date recurrences.

A — the three cases, warm-up

A1 $T(n)=2T(n/2)+n$
$W=n^{\log_2 2}=n$; $f=n=\Theta(n\lg^0 n)$ — exact match, case 2 ($k=0$). $\Theta(n\lg n)$ — merge sort.
A2 $T(n)=8T(n/2)+n^2$
$W=n^{\log_2 8}=n^3$; $f=n^2=O(n^{3-\varepsilon})$ — polynomially smaller, case 1. $\Theta(n^3)$.
A3 $T(n)=2T(n/2)+n^3$
$W=n$; $f=n^3=\Omega(n^{1+\varepsilon})$ — case 3. Regularity: $2f(n/2)=2\cdot n^3/8=n^3/4\le\frac14 f(n)$ ✓. $\Theta(n^3)$.
A4 $T(n)=T(n/2)+\Theta(1)$
$W=n^{\log_2 1}=n^0=1$; $f=\Theta(1)$ — exact match, case 2 ($k=0$). $\Theta(\lg n)$ — binary search.
A5 $T(n)=7T(n/2)+n^2$
$W=n^{\log_2 7}\approx n^{2.807}$; $f=n^2$ polynomially smaller — case 1. $\Theta(n^{\log_2 7})$ — Strassen's matrix multiplication.
A6 $T(n)=3T(n/2)+n$
$W=n^{\log_2 3}\approx n^{1.585}$; $f=n$ polynomially smaller — case 1. $\Theta(n^{\log_2 3})$ — Karatsuba multiplication.
A7 $T(n)=3T(n/4)+n\lg n$
$W=n^{\log_4 3}\approx n^{0.793}$; $f=n\lg n=\Omega(n^{0.793+\varepsilon})$ — case 3. Regularity: $3f(n/4)=\frac34 n(\lg n-2)\le\frac34 f(n)$ ✓. $\Theta(n\lg n)$.

B — the case-2 family (what exam 1 actually was)

B1 $T(n)=16T(n/4)+n^2$ EXAM 1
$W=n^{\log_4 16}=n^2$ — exact match ($k=0$), case 2. $\Theta(n^2\lg n)$.
B2 $T(n)=4T(n/2)+n^2\lg n$ EXAM 1
$W=n^{\log_2 4}=n^2$; $f=\Theta(n^2\lg^1 n)$ — extended case 2, $k=1$. $\Theta(n^2\lg^2 n)$.
B3 $T(n)=2T(n/4)+\sqrt n$ EXAM 1
$W=n^{\log_4 2}=n^{1/2}$ — exact match ($k=0$), case 2. $\Theta(\sqrt n\,\lg n)$.
B4 $T(n)=2T(n/2)+n\lg n$
$W=n$; $f=\Theta(n\lg^1 n)$ — $k=1$. $\Theta(n\lg^2 n)$.
B5 $T(n)=9T(n/3)+n^2\lg^2 n$
$W=n^{\log_3 9}=n^2$; $k=2$. $\Theta(n^2\lg^3 n)$.
B6 $T(n)=4T(n/2)+\dfrac{n^2}{\lg n}$
$W=n^2$; $f=\Theta(n^2\lg^{-1}n)$ — the $k=-1$ row. $\Theta(n^2\lg\lg n)$.

C — beyond the vanilla theorem

C1 $T(n)=3T(n/3)+\dfrac{n}{\lg n}$ EXAM 1
$W=n$; $f=\Theta(n\lg^{-1}n)$: cases 1–3 all fail ($f$ is smaller than $W$ but not polynomially). The $k=-1$ row gives $\Theta(n\lg\lg n)$. Akra–Bazzi check: $p=1$, $\int_2^n\frac{du}{u\lg u}=\Theta(\lg\lg n)$ ✓.
C2 $T(n)=2T(n/2)+\dfrac{n}{\lg^2 n}$
$W=n$; $k=-2<-1$ — the level sum $\sum_j 1/j^2$ converges, so the driving costs add only $O(n)$. $\Theta(n)$.
C3 $T(n)=T(n/3)+T(2n/3)+n$
Unequal split — Akra–Bazzi. $(1/3)^p+(2/3)^p=1\Rightarrow p=1$; $\int_1^n u\cdot u^{-2}du=\ln n$. $\Theta(n\lg n)$. (Recursion-tree view: every level costs $n$, depth $\Theta(\lg n)$.)
C4 $T(n)=T(n/5)+T(7n/10)+n$
The SELECT recurrence. At $p=1$: $\frac15+\frac{7}{10}=0.9<1$, so the balancing $p<1$. Then $\int_1^n u^{-p}du=\Theta(n^{1-p})$ and $T=\Theta(n^p\cdot n^{1-p})$. $\Theta(n)$ — the sum of subproblem sizes is $<n$, so the top level dominates.
C5 $T(n)=2T(n/2)+\dfrac{n^2}{\lg n}$
$W=n$; $f=n^2/\lg n=\Omega(n^{1+\varepsilon})$ (e.g. $\ge n^{1.5}$ eventually) — case 3. Regularity: $2f(n/2)=\frac{n^2/2}{\lg n-1}\le c\,\frac{n^2}{\lg n}$ with $c\to\frac12$ ✓. $\Theta\!\big(n^2/\lg n\big)$ — case 3 answers $\Theta(f)$, log factor and all.
C6 $T(n)=T(n/2)+T(n/3)+T(n/6)+\Theta(n\lg n)$ SHEET 2 · 26a
The fractions are chosen so the balance equation is trivial: $\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\tfrac16=1$, so $p=1$. Then $\int_2^n \frac{u\lg u}{u^{2}}\,du=\int_2^n\frac{\lg u}{u}\,du=\Theta(\lg^2 n)$. $T(n)=\Theta(n\lg^2 n)$. Recognise the "fractions summing to 1" giveaway — it means every level costs the same and $p=1$.
C7 $T(n)=3T(n/3)+8T(n/4)+\Theta\!\big(n^2/\lg n\big)$ SHEET 2 · 26b
Balance: $3\cdot3^{-p}+8\cdot4^{-p}=1$. At $p=1$ the left side is $1+2=3>1$; at $p=2$ it is $\tfrac13+\tfrac12=\tfrac56<1$; so $p\approx1.85$ — no closed form, and none is expected. Since the driving function $n^{s}/\lg n$ has $s=2>p$, the integral $\int^n u^{\,s-p-1}/\lg u\,du$ is dominated by its upper limit and evaluates to $\Theta\!\big(n^{s-p}/\lg n\big)$. Multiplying by $n^p$: $T(n)=\Theta\!\big(n^2/\lg n\big)$ — the root term dominates, the Akra–Bazzi analogue of Master case 3.

D — not Master-shaped at all (incl. change of variables)

Technique — change of variables (problems 28–29) When the recursion shrinks $n$ by a root rather than a factor, substitute $m=\lg n$ (so $n=2^m$) and set $S(m)=T(2^m)$. A $\sqrt n$ becomes $2^{m/2}$, i.e. $m\mapsto m/2$ — and the recurrence turns into an ordinary Master-Theorem one in $m$. Solve for $S$, then substitute back $m=\lg n$.

Worked, the professor's own example: $T(n)=2T(\sqrt n)+\Theta(\lg n)$. With $n=2^m$: $S(m)=T(2^m)=2T(2^{m/2})+\Theta(m)=2S(m/2)+\Theta(m)$. Master case 2 gives $S(m)=\Theta(m\lg m)$, hence $$T(n)=S(\lg n)=\Theta(\lg n\cdot \lg\lg n).$$
D1 $T(n)=T(n-1)+n$
Subtractive, not divide-and-conquer: expand. $T(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n k+O(1)$. $\Theta(n^2)$ — worst-case quicksort.
D2 $T(n)=2T(n-1)+1$
Expand: $T(n)=2^n T(0)+2^{n-1}+\cdots+1$. $\Theta(2^n)$ — Tower of Hanoi.
D3 $T(n)=T(\sqrt n)+1$
Substitute $m=\lg n$, $S(m)=T(2^m)$: then $S(m)=S(m/2)+1=\Theta(\lg m)$. $\Theta(\lg\lg n)$.
D4 $T(n)=2T(\sqrt n)+\Theta(1)$ SHEET 2 · 29a
$m=\lg n$: $S(m)=2S(m/2)+\Theta(1)$. Watershed $m^{\log_2 2}=m$, driving $\Theta(1)$ is polynomially smaller → case 1, $S(m)=\Theta(m)$. $T(n)=\Theta(\lg n)$.
D5 $T(n)=3T(\sqrt[3]{n})+\Theta(n)$ SHEET 2 · 29b
Here $\sqrt[3]{n}=2^{m/3}$, so $S(m)=3S(m/3)+\Theta(2^m)$. The driving term $2^m$ is exponential in $m$ and dwarfs the watershed $m^{\log_3 3}=m$: case 3 (regularity holds since $3\cdot2^{m/3}\le c\,2^m$ for large $m$). $S(m)=\Theta(2^m)$, so $T(n)=\Theta(n)$ — the top-level work dominates completely.
D6 $T(n)=3T(\sqrt n)+3T(\sqrt[3]{n})+\Theta((\lg\lg n)^2)$ SHEET 2 · 29c
$m=\lg n$ turns it into $S(m)=3S(m/2)+3S(m/3)+\Theta((\lg m)^2)$ — unequal splits, so Akra–Bazzi on $S$. Solve $3(1/2)^p+3(1/3)^p=1$; at $p=2$ the left side is $3/4+1/3=13/12>1$ and at $p=3$ it is $3/8+1/9\approx0.49<1$, so $p\in(2,3)$. Since $g(m)=\Theta(\lg^2 m)$ is negligible against $m^p$, the integral converges and $S(m)=\Theta(m^p)$. $T(n)=\Theta\big((\lg n)^p\big)$ with $3\cdot2^{-p}+3\cdot3^{-p}=1$. Leaving $p$ implicit is the expected answer.
