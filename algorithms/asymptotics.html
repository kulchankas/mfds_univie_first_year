Asymptotics & growth

The five symbols

All five are statements about eventual behaviour, up to constant factors. Each is a set of functions; $f=O(g)$ is an abuse of notation for $f\in O(g)$.

The definitions — learn these verbatim
$f=O(g)$ — at most, $\le$
$\exists c>0\ \exists n_0>0\ \forall n\ge n_0:\ 0\le f(n)\le c\,g(n)$
$f=\Omega(g)$ — at least, $\ge$
$\exists c>0\ \exists n_0>0\ \forall n\ge n_0:\ 0\le c\,g(n)\le f(n)$
$f=\Theta(g)$ — exactly, $=$
$\exists c_1,c_2>0\ \exists n_0>0\ \forall n\ge n_0:\ 0\le c_1g(n)\le f(n)\le c_2g(n)$
$f=o(g)$ — strictly less, $\lt$
$\forall c>0$ $\ \exists n_0>0\ \forall n\ge n_0:\ 0\le f(n)\lt c\,g(n)$
$f=\omega(g)$ — strictly greater, $\gt$
$\forall c>0$ $\ \exists n_0>0\ \forall n\ge n_0:\ 0\le c\,g(n)\lt f(n)$
The one structural fact $O$ and $\Omega$ say "there exists a constant $c$"; $o$ and $\omega$ say "for every constant $c$". That single quantifier flip is the whole difference between "at most" and "strictly less" — and it is why $o$ is a much stronger claim than $O$.

The pair $(c,n_0)$ is called a witness. To prove a bound you must produce one; you never have to find the best one.

The relationships
  • $f=\Theta(g)\iff f=O(g)\ \textbf{and}\ f=\Omega(g)$ — the standard way to prove a $\Theta$.
  • $f=o(g)\Rightarrow f=O(g)$, but not conversely. Likewise $\omega\Rightarrow\Omega$.
  • $f=o(g)\iff g=\omega(f)$, and $f=O(g)\iff g=\Omega(f)$ — transpose symmetry.
  • $f=o(g)\Rightarrow f\ne\Theta(g)$. The classes $o(g)$, $\Theta(g)$, $\omega(g)$ are pairwise disjoint.

The negations — you need these to disprove

Half of sheet 1 asks you to disprove something Negate by flipping every quantifier: $$f\ne O(g)\iff \forall c>0\ \forall n_0>0\ \exists n\ge n_0:\ f(n)>c\,g(n).$$ In words: for every candidate constant, the bound fails infinitely often. The proof shape is therefore "let $c$ be arbitrary; I exhibit arbitrarily large $n$ with $f(n)>c\,g(n)$" — usually by solving that inequality for $n$.

Similarly $f\ne o(g)$ means there is some $c>0$ such that $f(n)\ge c\,g(n)$ infinitely often — to disprove a little-o you only need to defeat one constant.

Proving and disproving, in practice

To prove $f=O(g)$ — produce witnesses

Claim: $3n^2+10n+7=O(n^2)$. For all $n\ge1$ we have $n\le n^2$ and $1\le n^2$, so $$3n^2+10n+7\ \le\ 3n^2+10n^2+7n^2\ =\ 20n^2 .$$ Witnesses $c=20$, $n_0=1$. ∎ — Note the technique: inflate every lower-order term up to the leading one. It is crude, it is legal, and it is fast.

To prove $f=\Omega(g)$ — discard, don't inflate

Claim: $3n^2+10n+7=\Omega(n^2)$. All terms are positive for $n\ge1$, so $3n^2+10n+7\ge 3n^2$. Witnesses $c=3$, $n_0=1$. ∎

With both directions, $3n^2+10n+7=\Theta(n^2)$.

To disprove — negate, then solve for $n$

Claim: $n^2\ne O(n)$. Let $c>0$ be arbitrary. Then $n^2>c\,n\iff n>c$. So for every $n_0$, taking $n=\max(n_0,\lceil c\rceil+1)$ gives $n\ge n_0$ and $n^2>cn$. The bound fails for every constant. ∎

Exam habit Write the definition you are using at the top of the answer before you start. It costs one line, it forces you to pick the right quantifier structure, and graders give partial credit for a correctly stated definition even when the algebra goes wrong.

The growth ladder

Memorise this order. Problems 14–15 are exactly "sort these functions", and almost every comparison you will ever need is an instance of one of these steps.

Strictly increasing growth — each $\ll$ means little-o $$1\ \ll\ \lg^\ast n\ \ll\ \lg\lg n\ \ll\ \lg n\ \ll\ \lg^k n\ \ll\ n^\varepsilon\ \ll\ n\ \ll\ n\lg n\ \ll\ n^2\ \ll\ n^c\ \ll\ c^n\ \ll\ n!\ \ll\ n^n$$ for any constants $k>0$, $\varepsilon>0$, $c>1$.
The three rules that generate the whole ladder 1. Any polylog beats any polynomial: $\lg^k n=o(n^\varepsilon)$ for every $k$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ — even $\lg^{100}n = o(n^{0.01})$.
2. Any polynomial beats any exponential: $n^k=o(c^n)$ for every $k$ and every $c>1$.
3. Exponentials with different bases are not comparable by constants: $2^n=o(3^n)$, and even $2^n = o(2^{2n})$.

$\lg^\ast n$iterated logarithmNumber of times you apply $\lg$ before reaching $\le1$. Grows absurdly slowly: $\lg^\ast(2^{65536})=5$. Problem 17.

$n\lg n$—Merge sort, heapsort; the comparison-sort lower bound.

What you may and may not do

Valid
  • Transitivity — all five symbols: $f=O(g)$ and $g=O(h)$ $\Rightarrow f=O(h)$.
  • Reflexivity — $O$, $\Omega$, $\Theta$ only: $f=\Theta(f)$. (Not $o$: $f\ne o(f)$.)
  • Symmetry — $\Theta$ only: $f=\Theta(g)\iff g=\Theta(f)$.
  • Sum rule — $O(f)+O(g)=O(\max(f,g))$; in particular $f+g=\Theta(\max(f,g))$ for non-negative $f,g$.
  • Product rule — $f_1=O(g_1)$ and $f_2=O(g_2)$ $\Rightarrow f_1f_2=O(g_1g_2)$.
  • Constants vanish — $\Theta(cf)=\Theta(f)$ for any constant $c>0$; and $\log_a n=\Theta(\log_b n)$, so log bases are irrelevant inside $\Theta$.
Invalid — these are the marks people lose
  • $O$ is not symmetric. $n=O(n^2)$ but $n^2\ne O(n)$.
  • No subtraction. $f=O(h)$ and $g=O(h)$ tells you nothing about $f-g$.
  • No trichotomy. Unlike real numbers, two functions need not be comparable at all — $f(n)=n^{1+\sin n}$ is neither $O(n)$ nor $\Omega(n)$.
  • Exponentiation does not respect $\Theta$. $2n=\Theta(n)$ but $2^{2n}=4^n\ne\Theta(2^n)$. Constant factors in an exponent are not constant factors.
  • Logs do not either, in the other direction: $\lg(n^2)=2\lg n=\Theta(\lg n)$ — here the constant does come out. Know which case you are in.

Standard estimates

Stirling's approximation MUST KNOW $$n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^{n}\left(1+\Theta\!\left(\frac1n\right)\right)$$ and the consequence you actually use: $$\lg(n!)=\Theta(n\lg n).$$ This is the step that turns "a decision tree needs $n!$ leaves" into the $\Omega(n\lg n)$ sorting lower bound.
If Stirling deserts you You can get the same bound by hand. Upper: $n!\le n^n$, so $\lg(n!)\le n\lg n$. Lower: at least half the factors of $n!$ are $\ge n/2$, so $n!\ge (n/2)^{n/2}$ and $\lg(n!)\ge\frac n2\lg\frac n2=\Omega(n\lg n)$. Two lines, no memorisation.

The limit test — the fastest tool you have

When both functions are positive and the limit exists, everything collapses to one computation:

Ratio test $$L=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \begin{cases} L=0 & f=o(g)\ \ (\text{so also } O)\\ 0<L<\infty & f=\Theta(g)\\ L=\infty & f=\omega(g)\ \ (\text{so also } \Omega) \end{cases}$$
Two cautions 1. If the limit does not exist the test says nothing — and that is exactly the case in the oscillating counterexamples above. Then you must go back to the definition.
2. The limit test is a shortcut for finding the answer. If a problem says "prove from the definition", a limit computation does not answer the question asked.

Two techniques that make hard comparisons easy

  • Take logarithms. $f\ll g\iff \lg f\ll \lg g$ when both grow. To compare $n^{\lg n}$ with $(\lg n)^n$: logs are $\lg^2 n$ and $n\lg\lg n$. The second is bigger, so $(\lg n)^n$ wins.
  • Substitute to kill the logs. Put $n=2^m$. Then $\lg n=m$, and a comparison of $\lg^{\lg n} n$ against $n$ becomes $m^{\lg m}$ against $2^m$ — a form you already know.

Traps that cost marks

TrapReality
"$f=O(g)$, so $g=O(f)$"$O$ is not symmetric. Only $\Theta$ is.
"$2^{2n}=\Theta(2^n)$, the 2 is a constant"$2^{2n}=4^n$. A constant in an exponent is a base change, not a factor.
"$\lg(n!)=\Theta(\lg(n^n))$? Surely not"Actually yes — both are $\Theta(n\lg n)$. Stirling, then $\lg(n^n)=n\lg n$.
"$n/\lg n$ is polynomially smaller than $n$"No. There is no $\varepsilon>0$ with $n/\lg n=O(n^{1-\varepsilon})$. This is why Master case 1 fails there — the single most expensive misconception in unit 3.
"$\sqrt n$ is basically $\lg n$"$\lg n=o(\sqrt n)$, by a wide margin. $\sqrt n$ is $n^{1/2}$ — a polynomial.
"Which log base?"Irrelevant inside $O/\Theta/\Omega$ (bases differ by a constant) — but not irrelevant in an exponent: $n^{\log_2 3}\ne n^{\log_3 2}$.
Forgetting $f$ must be eventually non-negativeThe definitions all require $0\le f(n)$ for $n\ge n_0$. Worth one sentence when $f$ could be negative.

Where this is examined

Sheet 1 problems 1, 9–14, 17 are pure asymptotics; 15–16 connect to recurrences. Problem 14/15 has a fully worked solution — treat it as the model for how much justification an ordering answer needs.

On the exam this material rarely appears as its own question. It appears inside every other question: question 2 is four recurrences (unit 3), and the big proof is often the $\Omega(n\lg n)$ bound, which is Stirling plus counting. Getting fluent here pays across the whole paper.
