Linearity and the indicator method

The whole trick Almost every expected-running-time proof in this course is the same move: write the quantity you care about as a sum of indicator variables, then use linearity of expectation. Linearity does not require independence — that is what makes it so powerful.
Indicator random variable For an event $A$, define $I\{A\}=1$ if $A$ occurs and $0$ otherwise. Then $$\mathbb{E}[I\{A\}] = 1\cdot\Pr[A] + 0\cdot\Pr[\bar A] = \Pr[A].$$ Expectation of an indicator is just the probability. Combined with $\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_i X_i\big]=\sum_i\mathbb{E}[X_i]$, counting problems become probability problems.

Worked pattern — expected number of comparisons in randomised quicksort: let $X_{ij}=I\{z_i\text{ compared with }z_j\}$. Then $X=\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i<j}\Pr[z_i\text{ vs }z_j]=\sum_{i<j}\frac{2}{j-i+1}$. The individual $X_{ij}$ are wildly dependent — linearity does not care. Full proof: Theorem 7.4.

Markov's inequality PROB 34

Markov's inequality Let $X\ge0$ be a random variable with $\mathbb{E}[X]$ defined. Then for every $t>0$, $$\Pr[X\ge t]\ \le\ \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{t}.$$

Proof (the one-line version — learn this one). Put $I=I\{X\ge t\}$. I claim $t\,I\le X$ always:

  • if $I=1$ then $X\ge t$, so $tI=t\le X$;
  • if $I=0$ then $tI=0\le X$, because $X\ge0$.

Taking expectations of $tI\le X$ and using monotonicity plus $\mathbb{E}[I]=\Pr[X\ge t]$: $$t\Pr[X\ge t]=\mathbb{E}[tI]\ \le\ \mathbb{E}[X].$$ Divide by $t>0$. $\blacksquare$

Where the hypotheses bite $X\ge0$ is essential — without it the second bullet fails (take $X=-100$ with certainty and $t=1$: $\Pr[X\ge1]=0$ but $\mathbb{E}[X]/t=-100$, and the inequality is vacuous or wrong in the general form). Markov is tight: for $X$ equal to $t$ with probability $p$ and $0$ otherwise, $\Pr[X\ge t]=p=\mathbb{E}[X]/t$ exactly. So no better bound exists using only the mean.

Chebyshev, in two lines from Markov

Apply Markov to the nonnegative variable $(X-\mu)^2$, where $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]$: $$\Pr\big[|X-\mu|\ge t\big]=\Pr\big[(X-\mu)^2\ge t^2\big]\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{t^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Var}[X]}{t^2}.$$ That is Chebyshev's inequality. It is the standard tool for problem 33 (the 40d6 fireball): with $\mathbb{E}=140$ and $\operatorname{Var}=40\cdot\frac{35}{12}$, Chebyshev bounds the chance of landing far from 140.

The geometric distribution PROB 35

Definition Repeat independent trials, each succeeding with probability $p$; let $X$ be the number of trials up to and including the first success. With $q=1-p$, $$\Pr[X=k]=q^{k-1}p\quad(k=1,2,\dots),\qquad \mathbb{E}[X]=\frac1p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}[X]=\frac{q}{p^2}.$$

Proof of the mean and variance by conditioning on the first trial. This is much cleaner than summing series. The first trial either succeeds (probability $p$, and $X=1$) or fails (probability $q$, and the process restarts, so $X=1+X'$ with $X'$ an independent copy).

Mean. $\mathbb{E}[X]=p\cdot1+q\cdot(1+\mathbb{E}[X])$, so $\mathbb{E}[X](1-q)=1$, giving $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/p$.

Second moment. Same conditioning, squaring: $$\mathbb{E}[X^2]=p\cdot1^2+q\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[(1+X)^2\big]=p+q\big(1+2\mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[X^2]\big).$$ Using $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/p$ and collecting the $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ terms: $$\mathbb{E}[X^2](1-q)=p+q+\frac{2q}{p}=1+\frac{2q}{p}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[X^2]=\frac1p+\frac{2q}{p^2}=\frac{p+2q}{p^2}=\frac{2-p}{p^2}.$$ Variance. $\operatorname{Var}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2=\dfrac{2-p}{p^2}-\dfrac{1}{p^2}=\dfrac{1-p}{p^2}=\dfrac{q}{p^2}$. $\blacksquare$

Sanity check At $p=1$ (always succeeds) the mean is 1 and the variance 0. ✓ As $p\to0$ the mean blows up like $1/p$ and the variance like $1/p^2$ — the standard deviation is comparable to the mean, so the geometric distribution is very spread out. That is why "expected $n$ probes" for random search does not mean "about $n$ probes".

The harmonic number, and why $\lg$ keeps appearing

$H_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k=\ln n+\gamma+O(1/n)=\Theta(\lg n)$. It shows up whenever you sum $1/k$ over a shrinking or growing set — the quicksort comparison sum, the coupon-collector count, and the successful-search bound for open addressing all reduce to it. Standard bound: $\ln(n+1)\le H_n\le 1+\ln n$, provable by comparing the sum with $\int\frac{dx}{x}$.

The three bounds worth carrying into the exam:

Sketch for the central binomial. Stirling gives $m!=\sqrt{2\pi m}\,(m/e)^m(1+\Theta(1/m))$. Then $$\binom{2n}{n}=\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi n}\,(2n/e)^{2n}}{2\pi n\,(n/e)^{2n}}\big(1+O(1/n)\big)=\frac{4^{n}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\big(1+O(1/n)\big).$$ Sketch for 30(b). $\binom nk=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}$; each of the $k$ factors is at least $n-k\ge n-\sqrt n$, and for $k\le\sqrt n$ the product $\prod_{i<k}(1-i/n)$ stays above $1/4$, which gives the constant.

Conditional independence PROB 32

Definition $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$ if $\Pr[A\cap B\mid C]=\Pr[A\mid C]\Pr[B\mid C]$. This is neither implied by nor implies ordinary independence.

An example where $A,B$ are dependent but conditionally independent given $C$ (this is what problem 32 asks for). Pick a coin at random: coin $C$ = "the fair coin was chosen" with probability $\tfrac12$, otherwise a two-headed coin. Flip the chosen coin twice; let $A$ = "first flip heads", $B$ = "second flip heads".

  • Given $C$ (we know which coin), the flips are independent by construction: $\Pr[A\cap B\mid C]=\tfrac14=\Pr[A\mid C]\Pr[B\mid C]$. ✓
  • Unconditionally, $\Pr[A]=\Pr[B]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac34$, while $\Pr[A\cap B]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac14+\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac58$. Since $\tfrac58\neq\tfrac9{16}$, they are dependent. ✗

The intuition: seeing the first flip come up heads is evidence that you are holding the two-headed coin, which then makes a second head more likely. Conditioning on $C$ removes exactly that channel of information.

The three searches PROB 44–46

Three ways to find $x$ in an unsorted array of $n$ elements, each analysed for one match, $k$ matches, and no match. This is the single best expected-value workout on the sheets.

Random search, one match. Each pick hits the target with probability $1/n$, independently — so the number of picks is geometric with $p=1/n$ and the expectation is $1/p=n$. With $k$ matches, $p=k/n$ and the answer is $n/k$.

Random search, no match. Now you must touch every index, sampling with replacement — the coupon collector problem. Splitting by "how long to find the $i$-th new index once $i-1$ are known", each phase is geometric with $p=(n-i+1)/n$, so $$\mathbb{E}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{n}{n-i+1}=n\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac1j=nH_n=\Theta(n\lg n).$$

Deterministic search, $k$ matches. With the $k$ matching positions uniformly placed among $n$ slots, the expected position of the first is $\frac{n+1}{k+1}$. (Check the ends: $k=1$ gives $\frac{n+1}{2}$ ✓; $k=n$ gives $1$ ✓.)

The question behind the question The sheet asks which would you use? Answer: deterministic search. It matches scramble search's expectation without paying $\Theta(n)$ to shuffle, and it beats random search on every row — random search is worse because sampling with replacement wastes picks on indices it has already seen. Scramble search only earns its keep if an adversary chooses the input order, since shuffling destroys that advantage.

Three broken shufflers PROB 38–40

The correct algorithm is RANDOMIZE-IN-PLACE: swap $A[i]$ with a random element of $A[i..n]$. Each of the three variants below breaks it, and the arguments are all counting arguments.

PERMUTE-BY-CYCLE — every element uniform, yet not a uniform permutation

Rotate the array by a single random offset. Each element genuinely does land in each position with probability $1/n$ — so the naive test passes. But the procedure can only produce the $n$ cyclic rotations, while there are $n!$ permutations. For $n\ge3$, $n<n!$, so most permutations occur with probability $0$.

The lesson "Every element is equally likely to be in every position" is a marginal condition and is strictly weaker than uniformity of the whole permutation. Any correctness proof must reason about permutations, not positions — which is exactly why Lemma 5.4's invariant is stated over $k$-permutations.

PERMUTE-WITHOUT-IDENTITY — right idea, wrong sample space

Swapping $A[i]$ with a random element of $A[i+1..n]$ for $i=1..n-1$ makes $\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(n-i)=(n-1)!$ equally likely execution paths. It does avoid the identity — but there are $n!-1$ non-identity permutations, and $(n-1)!\ne n!-1$ for $n\ge3$. So it cannot be uniform on the set it claims to sample.

PERMUTE-WITH-ALL — the divisibility argument

Swapping $A[i]$ with a random element of the whole array, for $i=1..n$, gives $n^n$ equally likely execution paths. If the output were uniform, each of the $n!$ permutations would arise from exactly $n^n/n!$ paths — so $n!$ would have to divide $n^n$. It does not: for $n=3$, $n^n=27$ is not divisible by $3!=6$. Hence non-uniform.

Exam-ready recipe To show a randomised procedure is not uniform, count execution paths $N$ and compare with $n!$: if $n!\nmid N$, you are done immediately, with no case analysis. To show one is uniform, use a loop invariant over $k$-permutations as in Lemma 5.4.
