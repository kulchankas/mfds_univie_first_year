The paper 40 pts · 90 min

1 · The differentiation pipeline 8 pts BLOCK 4

Question Let $f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x,y)=x^{2}y+e^{xy}$.
  1. Compute $\nabla f(x,y)$ and state $Df(x,y)$ — say clearly which is a row and which is a column. 3
  2. Give the affine approximation of $f$ at $a=(1,0)$. 3
  3. Compute the directional derivative of $f$ at $a$ in the direction $v=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$. 2
Solution — Q1

1 · Partials $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2xy+y\,e^{xy},\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=x^{2}+x\,e^{xy}.$$ The gradient is the column and the derivative is the row: $$\nabla f(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}2xy+ye^{xy}\\ x^{2}+xe^{xy}\end{pmatrix},\qquad Df(x,y)=\big(2xy+ye^{xy}\ \ \ x^{2}+xe^{xy}\big)=\nabla f(x,y)^{\mathsf T}.$$

2 · At $a=(1,0)$ Since $e^{xy}=e^{0}=1$ there, $$f(a)=1^{2}\cdot0+1=1,\qquad \nabla f(a)=\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot1\cdot0+0\cdot1\\ 1+1\cdot1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\2\end{pmatrix}.$$ The affine approximation is $T(x)=f(a)+Df(a)(x-a)$, i.e. writing $x=(1+h,\;0+k)$, $$\boxed{\,T(1+h,\,k)=1+\big(0\ \ 2\big)\begin{pmatrix}h\\k\end{pmatrix}=1+2k\,}$$ with $f(x)=T(x)+o(\lVert x-a\rVert)$ as $x\to a$ — that little-o clause is the definition of differentiability, so write it.

3 · Directional derivative For a differentiable $f$ and a unit vector $v$, $$\partial_v f(a)=\nabla f(a)\cdot v=\begin{pmatrix}0\\2\end{pmatrix}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{2}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2 .$$ $\partial_vf(a)=\sqrt2$

Two marks people drop here (1) Not checking $\lVert v\rVert=1$. If the direction is given un-normalised you must divide by its norm first, or the "derivative" is scaled by a factor. (2) Writing the gradient as a row. The guide flags this as the most common silent error — it only bites later, when you multiply matrices and the dimensions no longer line up.

2 · Hessian & classification 8 pts BLOCK 5

Question Let $f(x,y)=x^{3}-3xy+y^{3}$. Find all critical points and classify each one. State the criterion you use.
Solution — Q2

Step 1 — critical points: $\nabla f=0$ $$\nabla f=\begin{pmatrix}3x^{2}-3y\\ -3x+3y^{2}\end{pmatrix}=0\iff y=x^{2}\ \text{ and }\ x=y^{2}.$$ Substituting, $x=(x^{2})^{2}=x^{4}$, so $x^{4}-x=0$, i.e. $x(x^{3}-1)=0$, giving $x=0$ or $x=1$ (over $\mathbb{R}$). Hence $$\text{critical points: }(0,0)\ \text{ and }\ (1,1).$$

Step 2 — the Hessian $$H_f(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}6x&-3\\-3&6y\end{pmatrix}.$$

Step 3 — the criterion For a $2\times2$ Hessian at a critical point, with $D=\det H_f$:

  • $D>0$ and $(H_f)_{11}>0$ ⇒ local minimum (positive definite);
  • $D>0$ and $(H_f)_{11}<0$ ⇒ local maximum (negative definite);
  • $D<0$ ⇒ saddle (indefinite);
  • $D=0$ ⇒ the test is inconclusive.

Step 4 — evaluate

At $(0,0)$: $H_f=\begin{pmatrix}0&-3\\-3&0\end{pmatrix}$, $D=0\cdot0-(-3)^{2}=-9<0$ ⇒ saddle point.

At $(1,1)$: $H_f=\begin{pmatrix}6&-3\\-3&6\end{pmatrix}$, $D=36-9=27>0$ and $(H_f)_{11}=6>0$ ⇒ local minimum. (Eigenvalues $6\mp3$, i.e. $3$ and $9$ — both positive, confirming definiteness.)

Where this comes from The test is just Taylor to second order: $$f(a+h)=f(a)+\underbrace{\nabla f(a)\cdot h}_{=0}+\tfrac12h^{\mathsf T}H_f(a)h+o(\lVert h\rVert^{2}).$$ With the linear term gone, the sign of the quadratic form decides the local behaviour — which is why definiteness of $H_f$ is exactly the right question. Being able to say that sentence is must-prove result 4.
Show the recipe The guide is explicit that partial credit lives in the visible steps: write $\nabla f=0$ and its solutions, write $H_f$, then conclude. A bare answer of "saddle and minimum" scores far less than the same answer with three lines of working.

3 · Arc length 6 pts BLOCK 3

Question Compute the length of the curve $\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}$, $\gamma(t)=\big(t,\ \tfrac23 t^{3/2}\big)$.
Solution — Q3

1 · Differentiate componentwise $\gamma'(t)=\big(1,\ t^{1/2}\big)$.

2 · Norm — and simplify under the root first $$\lVert\gamma'(t)\rVert_2=\sqrt{1^{2}+\big(t^{1/2}\big)^{2}}=\sqrt{1+t}.$$

3 · Integrate $$L(\gamma)=\int_0^1\sqrt{1+t}\;dt=\left[\tfrac23(1+t)^{3/2}\right]_0^1=\tfrac23\big(2^{3/2}-1\big)=\frac{2}{3}\big(2\sqrt2-1\big).$$ $L=\tfrac23(2\sqrt2-1)\approx1.219$

The diagnostic The guide's rule: if the radicand isn't collapsing, recheck your derivative. Exam curves are rigged so that $\lVert\gamma'\rVert$ simplifies — here $t^{1/2}$ squares away to a clean $1+t$. A messy root almost always means an algebra slip upstream, not a hard integral.

4 · Lagrange multipliers 8 pts BLOCK 9

Question Find the maximum and minimum of $f(x,y,z)=x+2y+3z$ on the sphere $g(x,y,z)=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=14$. Justify that the extrema exist.
Solution — Q4

0 · Existence first The constraint set is closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, hence compact, and $f$ is continuous — so by the extreme value theorem $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum on it. (Saying this is worth a mark, and it is also what licenses you to just compare candidate values at the end.)

1 · Regularity $\nabla g=(2x,2y,2z)$ vanishes only at the origin, which is not on the sphere. So every constraint point is regular and the Lagrange condition applies.

2 · Set up $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ $$\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}2x\\2y\\2z\end{pmatrix}\;\Longrightarrow\;x=\frac{1}{2\lambda},\quad y=\frac{2}{2\lambda}=\frac1\lambda,\quad z=\frac{3}{2\lambda},$$ with $\lambda\ne0$ (if $\lambda=0$ the left side would be zero, which it is not).

3 · Impose the constraint $$x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=\frac{1+4+9}{4\lambda^{2}}=\frac{14}{4\lambda^{2}}=14\;\Longrightarrow\;\lambda^{2}=\frac14\;\Longrightarrow\;\lambda=\pm\tfrac12 .$$

4 · Candidates, and evaluate $f$ at each

  • $\lambda=\tfrac12$: $(x,y,z)=(1,2,3)$, and $f=1+4+9=14$.
  • $\lambda=-\tfrac12$: $(x,y,z)=(-1,-2,-3)$, and $f=-14$.

Comparing: maximum $14$ at $(1,2,3)$, minimum $-14$ at $(-1,-2,-3)$.

The classic way to lose this question Finding both candidates and then not evaluating $f$ at them — or evaluating and mislabelling which is which. The guide says it directly: leave 5 minutes to re-evaluate $f$ at each Lagrange candidate; forgetting to compare values is how max and min get swapped.
Sanity check By Cauchy–Schwarz, $$|f|=|(1,2,3)\cdot(x,y,z)|\le\lVert(1,2,3)\rVert\,\lVert(x,y,z)\rVert=\sqrt{14}\cdot\sqrt{14}=14,$$ with equality exactly when $(x,y,z)\parallel(1,2,3)$. That reproduces both the value and the maximiser in one line — a good way to check a Lagrange answer.

5 · Newton's method 6 pts BLOCK 8

Question Let $f(x)=x^{2}-2$.
  1. Write the Newton iteration for $f$ and perform two steps from $x_0=1$, as exact fractions. 4
  2. Justify in one or two sentences why Newton's method is "the root of the linearization". 2
Solution — Q5

1 · The iteration $f'(x)=2x$, so $$x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}=x_k-\frac{x_k^{2}-2}{2x_k}=\frac{x_k}{2}+\frac{1}{x_k}.$$

From $x_0=1$: $$x_1=\frac12+1=\frac32,\qquad x_2=\frac{3}{4}+\frac{2}{3}=\frac{9+8}{12}=\frac{17}{12}.$$ $x_1=\tfrac32$, $x_2=\tfrac{17}{12}\approx1.41667$ — against $\sqrt2\approx1.41421$, so two steps already give three correct digits.

2 · Newton as linearization Replace $f$ near $x_k$ by its affine approximation $$T(x)=f(x_k)+f'(x_k)(x-x_k),$$ and take $x_{k+1}$ to be the root of that: solving $T(x)=0$ gives $x=x_k-f(x_k)/f'(x_k)$, which is precisely the Newton step. So each iteration replaces the curve by its tangent line and jumps to where the tangent crosses the axis.

Worth one extra sentence if you have time Because the affine model has error $O(\lVert x-x_k\rVert^{2})$, the iteration converges quadratically near a simple root — the digit count roughly doubles each step, which the numbers above already show. Quadratic convergence needs $f'(x^{*})\ne0$; at a double root Newton degrades to linear.

6 · Julia: gradient descent 4 pts BLOCK 7

Question Write a Julia function implementing gradient descent with a fixed step size for a function $f$ with gradient ∇f, starting from x0. Comment the update line with the mathematics it implements.
Solution — Q6
function gradient_descent(∇f, x0; α=0.1, iters=1000, tol=1e-8)
    x = copy(x0)
    for k in 1:iters
        g = ∇f(x)
        if norm(g) < tol       # stationary point reached
            break
        end
        x = x - α * g          # x_{k+1} = x_k − α ∇f(x_k)
    end
    return x
end
How this is marked The guide is explicit: a correct loop skeleton with the right update line scores even if syntax is imperfect — write the math of the update as a comment. So the four things to get down, in order of value:
  1. the update $x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha\nabla f(x_k)$ — minus the gradient, because $-\nabla f$ is the steepest-descent direction;
  2. a loop with an iteration cap;
  3. a stopping test on $\lVert\nabla f(x_k)\rVert$;
  4. copy(x0) so you do not mutate the caller's vector.
If asked for SGD or mini-batch instead Only the gradient changes: SGD replaces $\nabla f$ by $\nabla f_i$ for a single randomly drawn $i$, and mini-batch by $\tfrac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}\nabla f_i$ over a random batch $B$. The loop and the update line are identical — say so, and you have answered all three variants.

The six must-prove results

Where this list comes from The study guide's day-16 task says "re-prove the 6 must-prove results aloud" but never says which six. This list is assembled from the guide's own emphasis — the results it marks Prove in the state/prove/compute framing, cross-checked against your professor's ✅ coding. It is a reasoned reconstruction, not a quotation. If your lecturer named a different six, theirs wins.
1 · Continuous on compact ⇒ uniformly continuous BLOCK 2 Statement. If $f:X\to Y$ is continuous and $X$ is compact, then $f$ is uniformly continuous.
Key idea. Contradiction plus sequential compactness: if uniform continuity fails, there is an $\varepsilon>0$ and points $x_n,x_n'$ with $d(x_n,x_n')\le1/n$ but $d(f(x_n),f(x_n'))>\varepsilon$. Extract a convergent subsequence $x_{n_k}\to x$; then $x_{n_k}'\to x$ too, and continuity at $x$ forces both images to $f(x)$ — contradicting the gap $\varepsilon$.
The one thing to remember: compactness is used only to extract the subsequence.
2 · The three convexity closure rules BLOCK 6 · PS7 P3 Statement. Convexity is preserved by (i) non-negative combinations $\alpha f+\beta g$ with $\alpha,\beta\ge0$; (ii) pointwise suprema $\sup_{i\in I}f_i$; (iii) pre-composition with an affine map, $x\mapsto f(Ax+b)$.
Key idea. All three are one line from the definition — no calculus, no Hessians. For (ii) the trick is that the sup of functions each satisfying the convexity inequality still satisfies it, because the inequality survives taking suprema on both sides.
Why it earns its place: it is how you prove a function convex in practice, since most objectives are built from simple pieces by exactly these operations.
3 · Neumann series BLOCK 10 · §7.5 Statement. If $\lVert A\rVert<1$ in a submultiplicative operator norm on a complete space, then $I-A$ is invertible, $$(I-A)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A^{k},\qquad\text{and}\qquad \big\lVert(I-A)^{-1}\big\rVert\le\frac{1}{1-\lVert A\rVert}.$$ Key idea. Submultiplicativity gives $\lVert A^{k}\rVert\le\lVert A\rVert^{k}$, so the series is dominated by a convergent geometric series and therefore converges absolutely (completeness turns that into convergence). Then the telescoping identity $(I-A)\sum_{k=0}^{n}A^{k}=I-A^{n+1}\to I$ identifies the limit.
Where it is used: perturbation bounds and the invertibility half of the inverse function theorem.
4 · The second-derivative test, from Taylor BLOCK 5 Statement. At a critical point $a$ ($\nabla f(a)=0$) of a $C^{2}$ function: $H_f(a)$ positive definite ⇒ strict local minimum; negative definite ⇒ strict local maximum; indefinite ⇒ saddle.
Key idea. Second-order Taylor kills the linear term: $$f(a+h)=f(a)+\tfrac12h^{\mathsf T}H_f(a)h+o(\lVert h\rVert^{2}),$$ so for small $h$ the sign of $f(a+h)-f(a)$ is the sign of the quadratic form. Definiteness gives a uniform bound $h^{\mathsf T}H h\ge\mu\lVert h\rVert^{2}$ with $\mu>0$ (the smallest eigenvalue), which dominates the $o(\lVert h\rVert^{2})$ remainder.
The subtlety: semi-definite is not enough — that is exactly the inconclusive case.
5 · Lagrange's necessary condition BLOCK 9 Statement. If $a$ is a local extremum of $f$ on $M=\{g=0\}$ and $a$ is a regular point ($Dg(a)$ has full rank), then $\nabla f(a)\in\operatorname{span}\{\nabla g_1(a),\dots,\nabla g_m(a)\}$ — i.e. $\nabla f(a)=\sum_i\lambda_i\nabla g_i(a)$.
Key idea. Regularity makes $M$ a submanifold, so every tangent vector $v\in T_aM$ is $\gamma'(0)$ for a curve $\gamma$ inside $M$. Then $t\mapsto f(\gamma(t))$ has an interior extremum at $0$, so its derivative $\nabla f(a)\cdot v$ vanishes for every tangent $v$. Hence $\nabla f(a)\perp T_aM=\ker Dg(a)$, and that orthogonal complement is spanned by the $\nabla g_i(a)$.
Do not forget: the condition is necessary, not sufficient, and it requires regularity — which is why Q4 above checks $\nabla g\ne0$ before using it.
6 · Newton's method converges quadratically BLOCK 8 Statement. If $f$ is $C^{2}$, $f(x^{*})=0$ and $f'(x^{*})\ne0$, then for $x_0$ close enough to $x^{*}$ Newton's iteration converges and $|x_{k+1}-x^{*}|\le C|x_k-x^{*}|^{2}$.
Key idea. Taylor-expand $f$ about $x_k$ and evaluate at $x^{*}$: $$0=f(x^{*})=f(x_k)+f'(x_k)(x^{*}-x_k)+\tfrac12f''(\xi)(x^{*}-x_k)^{2}.$$ Divide by $f'(x_k)$ and rearrange into the Newton step; the linear terms cancel and what is left is the squared error times $f''(\xi)/2f'(x_k)$.
Where each hypothesis is used: $f'(x^{*})\ne0$ keeps the denominator away from zero near the root (this is what fails at a double root); $C^{2}$ supplies the remainder term.
How to drill these One per day, out loud, from a blank page — statement first, then the key idea in one sentence, then the details. If you can say the key idea line for all six, you can rebuild every proof under exam pressure; if you can only recite details you will lose the thread the moment you are interrupted.
