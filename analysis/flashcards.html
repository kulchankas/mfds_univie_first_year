Define a metric $d$ on a set $X\neq\emptyset$, and when $(X,d)$ is a metric space.
$d:X\times X\to[0,\infty)$ is a metric if for all $x,y,z$: (M1) symmetry $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$; (M2) triangle inequality $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$; (M3) definiteness $d(x,y)=0\iff x=y$. Then $(X,d)$ is a metric space.
When does a sequence $(x_n)\subseteq X$ converge to $x$ in a metric space?
$x_n\to x$ iff $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N:\ d(x_n,x)\le\varepsilon$. Equivalently $d(x_n,x)\to 0$.
If $x_n\to x$ and $x_n\to x'$ in a metric space — conclusion, and the one-line proof?
Limits are unique: $x=x'$. Proof: $d(x,x')\le d(x,x_n)+d(x_n,x')\to 0$, so $d(x,x')=0$, hence $x=x'$.
Define order $r>1$, linear, and sublinear convergence of $x_k\to x$.
Order $r$: $\exists c>0:\ d(x_{k+1},x)\le c\,d(x_k,x)^r$.  Linear: $\exists c\in(0,1):\ d(x_{k+1},x)\le c\,d(x_k,x)$.  Sublinear: $\limsup_k d(x_k,x)^{1/k}=1$.
What is an isometry $f:X\to Y$?
$d_Y(f(x),f(x'))=d_X(x,x')$ for all $x,x'$ — it preserves distances.
When is a sequence (or subset) in a metric space bounded?
$\exists x\in X,\ C>0:\ d(x_n,x)\le C$ for all $n$ (resp. $d(s,x)\le C$ for all $s\in S$). The anchor point $x$ is arbitrary — changing it only changes $C$.
Why is every convergent sequence bounded?
Past some $N$, $d(x_n,x)<1$; then $d(x_n,x)\le\max\{d(x_1,x),\dots,d(x_{N-1},x),1\}$ for all $n$.
Give the taxicab, maximum, and discrete metrics.
Taxicab: $d_1(x,y)=|x_1-y_1|+|x_2-y_2|$.  Maximum: $d_{\max}(x,y)=\max\{|x_1-y_1|,|x_2-y_2|\}$.  Discrete: $d(x,y)=0$ if $x=y$, else $1$ (converges $\iff$ eventually constant).
In $\mathbb R$, when does monotonicity force convergence?
A sequence that is monotonically increasing and bounded above converges (to its supremum). Uses order-completeness of $\mathbb R$.
State the density of $\mathbb Q$ in $\mathbb R$.
For every $x\in\mathbb R$ there is $(x_n)\subseteq\mathbb Q$ with $x_n\to x$. We say $\mathbb Q$ is dense in $\mathbb R$.
Define a Cauchy sequence.
$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n,m\ge N:\ d(x_n,x_m)\le\varepsilon$ — terms get arbitrarily close to each other, no limit required.
Relationship between convergent and Cauchy sequences?
Every convergent sequence is Cauchy. (The converse holds only in complete spaces.)
When is a metric space complete?
When every Cauchy sequence converges to a limit inside the space.
Which of $\mathbb Q,\ \mathbb R,\ \mathbb C$ are complete?
$\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$ are complete; $\mathbb Q$ is not. Counterexample: the iteration $x_{n+1}=\tfrac12(x_n+2/x_n)$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb Q$ but $\to\sqrt 2\notin\mathbb Q$.
What is a subsequence, and how does it relate to convergence?
$(x_{n_j})_j$ via strictly increasing indices $n_0<n_1<\cdots$. If $x_n\to x$, then every subsequence $\to x$.
State Bolzano–Weierstraß.
Every bounded sequence in $\mathbb R$ has a convergent subsequence. (Proof by repeated interval bisection / nested intervals.)
Define (sequential) compactness of a metric space $X$.
Every sequence in $X$ has a subsequence converging to a limit in $X$ — sequences cannot "escape" the space.
Does compact imply complete?
Yes. A Cauchy sequence has a convergent subsequence (compactness), and a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.
Define the ball $B_\varepsilon(x)$ and the open (interior) ball $\mathring B_\varepsilon(x)$.
$B_\varepsilon(x)=\{y\in X: d(x,y)\le\varepsilon\}$ (this text's $B$ is the closed ball); $\mathring B_\varepsilon(x)=\{y: d(x,y)<\varepsilon\}$ (open).
Define open, closed, and neighborhood.
$S$ open: $\forall x\in S\ \exists\varepsilon>0:\ B_\varepsilon(x)\subseteq S$.  $S$ closed: $X\setminus S$ is open.  Neighborhood of $x$: a set containing some ball $B_\varepsilon(x)$. ($\emptyset$ and $X$ are both open and closed.)
Sequential characterization of a closed set $S$?
$S$ is closed $\iff$ it contains the limit of every convergent sequence $(x_n)\subseteq S$ with $x_n\to x\in X$.
What does compactness imply about a subset (any metric space)?
A compact subset is closed and bounded. (Unbounded ⇒ an "escape" sequence; not closed ⇒ a limit outside, both contradict compactness.)
State the Heine–Borel theorem in $\mathbb R$.
$K\subseteq\mathbb R$ (standard metric) is compact $\iff$ closed and bounded. E.g. $[a,b]$ is compact; $(0,1)$ is not.
For $S\subseteq\mathbb R$ bounded above, how does $\sup S$ relate to sequences in $S$?
There is a sequence $(x_n)\subseteq S$ with $x_n\to\sup S$ (dually for $\inf$). So the supremum/infimum of a set are limits of points of the set.
If $a_n\to a$ in $\mathbb R$ and $a_n\ge 0$ for all $n$ — what about $a$?
$a\ge 0$. Convergence respects non-strict inequalities (strict can fail: $1/n>0$ but $\to 0$).
If $a_n\to a$, $b_n\to b$ and $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n$ — conclusion?
$a\le b$. (Apply 1.1.18 to $c_n=b_n-a_n\ge 0$.)
Define the truncated metric $\tilde d=\min\{1,d\}$ and state what it preserves.
$\tilde d(x,y)=\min\{1,d(x,y)\}$ is a metric, and it preserves both convergence and compactness: $x_n\to x$ in $d\iff$ in $\tilde d$, and $K$ compact in $d\iff$ in $\tilde d$. (So every metric is equivalent to a bounded one — boundedness alone is not a metric invariant.)
Give two equivalent definitions of continuity of $f$ at $x$ (plus the global topological one).
Sequential: $x_n\to x\Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(x)$.  ($\varepsilon$–$\delta$): $\forall\varepsilon>0\,\exists\delta>0:\ d_X(x,x')<\delta\Rightarrow d_Y(f(x),f(x'))<\varepsilon$.  Global: $f$ continuous $\iff f^{-1}(U)$ open for every open $U$ $\iff f^{-1}(C)$ closed for every closed $C$.
Are compositions continuous? Is $\varphi_y(x)=d(x,y)$ continuous?
Yes — $g\circ f$ is continuous (sequential proof). And $\varphi_y(x)=d(x,y)$ is continuous because it is 1-Lipschitz: $|d(x_n,y)-d(x,y)|\le d(x_n,x)$.
If $X$ is compact and $f$ continuous, what about the image $f(X)$?
$f(X)$ is compact — the continuous image of a compact set is compact.
State the Extreme Value Theorem.
A continuous $f:X\to\mathbb R$ on a compact $X$ attains its max and min: $\exists p,q\in X$ with $f(p)=\max_X f$, $f(q)=\min_X f$.
Define path-connected; characterize path-connected subsets of $\mathbb R$.
Path-connected: any $x,y$ are joined by a continuous $\gamma:[0,1]\to X$ with $\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y$. For $X\subseteq\mathbb R$: path-connected $\iff$ $X$ is an interval. (Continuous images of path-connected sets are path-connected.)
State the Intermediate Value Theorem.
Continuous $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ has $f([a,b])$ a closed bounded interval; if $f(a)<0<f(b)$ then $\exists\xi\in(a,b)$ with $f(\xi)=0$. (Bisection: $|x_n-\hat x|\le 2^{-n}|b-a|$.)
State the Banach Fixed-Point Theorem with its error estimate.
On a complete space, a contraction $d(f(x),f(y))\le q\,d(x,y)$ with $q\in[0,1)$ has a unique fixed point $x^*$; the iterates $x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$ converge linearly with $d(x_n,x^*)\le \dfrac{q^{\,n}}{1-q}\,d(x_1,x_0)$.
Define uniform continuity and give the key compactness result.
$\forall\varepsilon>0\,\exists\delta>0\,\forall x_1,x_2:\ d_X(x_1,x_2)\le\delta\Rightarrow d_Y(f(x_1),f(x_2))\le\varepsilon$ — one $\delta$ works for all points. On a compact domain, continuous $\Rightarrow$ uniformly continuous.
What does uniform convergence preserve, and is $C_b(X,Y)$ complete?
A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (pointwise is not enough — $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$). The bounded continuous functions $C_b(X,Y)$ with $d_\infty(f,g)=\sup_x d_Y(f(x),g(x))$ are complete whenever $Y$ is.
What makes a sum telescoping, and what is $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n(n+1)}$?
The terms are consecutive differences, so everything interior cancels: $$\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(a_n-a_{n+1}\big)=a_1-a_{N+1}.$$ Partial fractions give $\frac{1}{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}$, so $S_N=1-\frac{1}{N+1}\to1$ and the series equals 1. The move is always the same: find the difference representation, then read off the two survivors.
Name four places outside §2.1 where these notes use a telescoping sum.
(1) Riemann integrability (p.82): $\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x_k-x_{k-1})=x_n-x_0=b-a$, which is what bounds $U(f,P)-L(f,P)\le\varepsilon$. (2) Neumann series (p.106): $f\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\mathrm{id}-f)^k=\mathrm{id}-(\mathrm{id}-f)^{n+1}$ — the step that identifies the inverse. (3) Multivariable differentiability (p.148): $f(x+h)-f(x)=\sum_{j}\big(f(x^{(j)})-f(x^{(j-1)})\big)$, reducing the argument to $n$ one-dimensional mean-value steps. (4) Gradient-descent convergence (p.160): summing $2\eta\big(f(x_k)-f(x^{*})\big)\le\lVert e_k\rVert^{2}-\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert^{2}+\eta^{2}C^{2}$ collapses the middle and leaves $\lVert e_0\rVert^{2}$.

It is a proof technique, not a series trick — that is why it is worth recognising.
State the finite geometric sum and the geometric series, with the exact convergence condition.
Finite, for $x\ne1$: $$\sum_{k=0}^{N}x^{k}=\frac{1-x^{N+1}}{1-x}.$$ If $|x|<1$ then $x^{N+1}\to0$, so $$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^{k}=\frac{1}{1-x},\qquad \forall x\in\mathbb{C},\ |x|<1 .$$ The identity holds in any ring with identity in which $1-x$ is invertible — which is exactly why the same algebra gives the Neumann series $(\mathrm{id}-f)^{-1}=\sum_k(\mathrm{id}-f)^{k}$ when $\lVert\mathrm{id}-f\rVert<1$ (p.106).
Does the harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n$ converge? Justify.
No — it diverges, in the notes' words "albeit very slowly".

Proof (dyadic blocks). Group the terms from $2^{k}+1$ to $2^{k+1}$: there are $2^{k}$ of them, each at least $\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}$, so every block sums to at least $$2^{k}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}=\frac12 .$$ There are infinitely many blocks, so the partial sums are unbounded: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n=\infty$.

The moral: $a_n\to0$ is necessary but not sufficient for $\sum a_n$ to converge — this is the standard counterexample.

The trap: the notes plot $S(n)$ for $n$ up to $10^{4}$ first, and it looks like it levels off. It does not — $S_n=\ln n+\gamma+o(1)$, so $S_{10^4}\approx9.79$ and you would need about $e^{100}$ terms to reach 100. Numerical evidence cannot establish convergence.
State the three convergence criteria for series.
For sequences $(a_n),(b_n)$ of complex numbers:

(1) Comparison test. If $|a_n|\le|b_n|$ for all $n$ and $\sum|b_n|$ converges, then $\sum|a_n|$ converges. (Partial sums are increasing and bounded above.)

(2) Ratio test (quotient criterion). If there exist $0<\theta<1$ and $N$ with $$\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|\le\theta\qquad\forall n\ge N,$$ then $\sum|a_n|$ converges. (Iterating gives $|a_n|\le\theta^{n}|a_0|$, then compare with the geometric series.)

(3) Leibniz criterion. If $(a_n)\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is non-negative, monotonically decreasing and $a_n\to0$, then the alternating series $\sum(-1)^{n}a_n$ converges.

Note (1) and (2) give absolute convergence; (3) does not.
The harmonic series has $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{n}{n+1}<1$ for every $n$. Why does the ratio test not prove it converges?
Because the test demands a single constant $\theta<1$ that works for all large $n$ — not merely that each ratio is $<1$. Here $$\frac{n}{n+1}\to1,$$ so no $\theta<1$ dominates the ratios eventually. The notes flag this explicitly: "the existence of a number $\theta<1$ … is critical."

The usable version (Remark 2.1.13): if $\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|\to q<1$, then any $\theta$ with $q<\theta<1$ works — so a limit strictly below 1 is enough, but a limit equal to 1 tells you nothing either way.
Define absolute convergence. What two things does it buy you?
$\sum a_n$ converges absolutely if $\sum|a_n|$ converges.

(i) It implies convergence (Lem 2.1.7). Proof: the partial sums of $\sum|a_n|$ are Cauchy, and $\big|\sum_{n=M+1}^{N}a_n\big|\le\sum_{n=M+1}^{N}|a_n|$, so the partial sums of $\sum a_n$ are Cauchy too — then use completeness.

(ii) Reordering does not change the value (Lem 2.1.8): for any bijection $\tau:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$, $\sum a_{\tau(n)}=\sum a_n$.

Why the course cares: in computation the summation order changes for reasons you do not control (parallelism, optimisation). Absolute convergence is what guarantees the answer does not.
Give a series that converges but not absolutely, and name both facts used.
The alternating harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}$.

Converges: $b_n=\frac1n$ is non-negative, decreasing and $\to0$, so the Leibniz criterion applies.

Not absolutely: $\sum\left|\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}\right|=\sum\frac1n$ is the harmonic series, which diverges.

This is the standard witness that convergence is strictly weaker than absolute convergence — and it is why Lemma 2.1.8's reordering guarantee needs the absolute hypothesis.
Why does absolute convergence play a central role?
Because of its invariance under reordering.

The theorem (Lem 2.1.8): if $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely, then for every bijection $\tau:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$, $$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{\tau(n)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n .$$ The value is a property of the collection of terms, not of the order you happen to add them in.

Why that matters (Rem 2.1.9, "robust aggregation"): in computation the summation order changes for reasons you do not control — parallel reduction, chunking, compiler and BLAS optimisation. Absolute convergence is precisely the hypothesis guaranteeing the answer does not change with it.

The hypothesis is sharp. Convergence alone is not enough: $\sum\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}$ converges by Leibniz but not absolutely (Ex 2.1.17), and for such conditionally convergent real series a reordering can be made to converge to any prescribed value — Riemann's rearrangement theorem. (Standard result; stated here for contrast, not proved in these notes.)

Note too that the comparison and ratio tests deliver absolute convergence, so it is the default mode you actually get.
Define the exponential function and $e$. Why does the series converge for every $z\in\mathbb{C}$?
$$\exp(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\qquad e:=\exp(1)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}.$$ Convergence — the ratio test, done properly. The ratio of consecutive terms is $$\frac{z^{n+1}/(n+1)!}{z^{n}/n!}=\frac{z}{n+1}.$$ Fix $z$ and choose $N_0\in\mathbb{N}$ with $|z|<N_0$. Then for all $n\ge N_0$, $$\frac{|z|}{n+1}\le\frac{|z|}{N_0+1}=:\theta<1 .$$ That is a uniform $\theta<1$, which is exactly what Thm 2.1.12(2) demands — so the series converges absolutely for every $z\in\mathbb{C}$.

Note the shape of the argument: the ratio $\to0$, so you can always manufacture the $\theta$ by starting late enough. Compare the harmonic series, where you cannot.
Prove the remainder estimate for $\exp$. Where is the hypothesis $|z|\le\frac{N+2}{2}$ used?
Statement. For every $z\in\mathbb{C}$ and $N\in\mathbb{N}$, $$\exp(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}+R_{N+1}(z),\qquad |R_{N+1}(z)|\le 2\,\frac{|z|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\quad\text{whenever }|z|\le\tfrac{N+2}{2}.$$ Proof — four estimates. Start from the tail and factor out the first term: $$|R_{N+1}(z)|\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{|z|^{n}}{n!} =\frac{|z|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\left(1+\frac{|z|}{N+2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{(N+2)(N+3)}+\cdots\right).$$ (1) Weaken each denominator: $(N+2)(N+3)\cdots\ge(N+2)^{k}$, so the bracket is at most $$1+\frac{|z|}{N+2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{(N+2)^{2}}+\cdots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{|z|}{N+2}\right)^{k}.$$ (2) Here is where the hypothesis enters: $|z|\le\frac{N+2}{2}$ is exactly $\dfrac{|z|}{N+2}\le\dfrac12$. (3) So the bracket is dominated by the geometric series $\sum_{k\ge0}\left(\tfrac12\right)^{k}=\dfrac{1}{1-\frac12}=2$. (4) Hence $|R_{N+1}(z)|\le 2\dfrac{|z|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}$. ∎

The constant $2$ is not decoration — it is the sum of that geometric series, and the radius $\frac{N+2}{2}$ is chosen precisely to force the ratio below $\tfrac12$.

What it buys: a computable error bar on the truncation. At $N=0$: $|\exp(z)-1|\le2|z|$ for $|z|\le1$, which is how continuity of $\exp$ at $0$ is established (Cor 2.2.7).
Use the remainder bound to show the truncations $f_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}$ converge uniformly to $\exp$ on $[-1,1]$. What does that give you?
On $[-1,1]$ we have $|x|\le1\le\frac{N+2}{2}$ for every $N\ge0$, so Thm 2.2.2 applies with a bound independent of $x$: $$\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}\big|f_N(x)-\exp(x)\big|\;\le\;\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}\frac{2|x|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\;\le\;\frac{2}{(N+1)!}\;\longrightarrow\;0 .$$ The bound does not depend on $x$ — that is exactly what makes the convergence uniform rather than merely pointwise.

Payoff: each $f_N$ is a polynomial, hence continuous; a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (Thm 1.4.7), so $\exp|_{[-1,1]}$ is continuous. Pointwise convergence would not have sufficed — compare $f_n(x)=x^{n}$ on $[0,1]$.

myExp(x,N) = sum(x^n/factorial(big(n)) for n=0:N)
R(x,N) = 2*abs(x)^(N+1)/factorial(big(N+1))
State the Cauchy product. What hypothesis does it need, and why?
If $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ are absolutely convergent, define $$c_n:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_k\,b_{n-k}.$$ Then $\sum c_n$ is absolutely convergent and $$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n=\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n\Big)\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_n\Big).$$ Why absolute convergence: the proof compares the square index set $\{0\le k,l\le N\}$ with the triangle $\{k+l\le N\}$ and bounds the difference by $\sum|a_k||b_l|$ over the gap. Only absolute convergence makes that tail vanish — this is the reordering-invariance of §2.1 doing real work.
State and prove the functional equation of $\exp$.
$$\exp(x+y)=\exp(x)\exp(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{C}.$$ Proof. Both series are absolutely convergent, so the Cauchy product (Lem 2.2.5) applies with $a_n=\frac{x^{n}}{n!}$, $b_n=\frac{y^{n}}{n!}$: $$c_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{y^{\,n-k}}{(n-k)!}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}y^{\,n-k}=\frac{(x+y)^{n}}{n!},$$ the middle step being the binomial theorem. Summing, $\exp(x)\exp(y)=\sum_n c_n=\exp(x+y)$. ∎

This one identity generates almost everything else: $\exp(-z)=1/\exp(z)$, $\exp(k)=e^{k}$, the logarithm's $\log(xy)=\log x+\log y$, and the trigonometric addition formulas.
List the four properties of $\exp$ that follow from the functional equation, and how continuity is obtained.
(0) $\exp:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ is continuous. First at $0$: the $N=0$ remainder gives $|\exp(z)-1|\le2|z|$ for $|z|\le1$, so $\exp(z)\to1=\exp(0)$. Then everywhere, by the functional equation: if $z_n\to z$, $$\exp(z_n)=\exp(z)\exp(z_n-z)\to\exp(z)\exp(0)=\exp(z).$$ (i) $\exp(-z)=\dfrac{1}{\exp(z)}$  — from $\exp(z)\exp(-z)=\exp(0)=1$. In particular $\exp$ never vanishes.
(ii) $\exp(x)>0$ for all real $x$ — for $x\ge0$ read it off the series ($\ge1$); for $x<0$ use (i).
(iii) $\exp(k)=e^{k}$ for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ — induction upward, then (i) for negatives.

Pattern to notice: continuity at a single point plus the functional equation upgrades to continuity everywhere. That trick recurs.
A series defines a function by $f(x):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_n(x)$. Why does convergence make $f$ well-defined — and what does it not give you?
A series is not a formula you can simply write down; it is a limit of partial sums: $$f(x):=\lim_{N\to\infty}S_N(x),\qquad S_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}f_n(x).$$ "Well-defined" means the recipe assigns exactly one value to each $x$. Three things have to hold, and convergence supplies the first two:

(1) Existence. If $S_N(x)$ does not converge, there is no value to assign and $f$ is simply undefined at $x$. So convergence determines the domain: $\sum x^{n}$ defines a function on $|x|<1$ and nowhere else, while $\sum\frac{z^{n}}{n!}$ converges for every $z\in\mathbb{C}$ — which is exactly why $\exp:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ is legitimate.
(2) Uniqueness. Limits in $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ are unique when they exist, so the value is unambiguous — for a fixed ordering of the terms.
(3) Independence of arbitrary choices. Summing infinitely many terms presupposes an enumeration. If reordering changed the answer, $f$ would depend on the enumeration you happened to pick rather than on the collection $\{f_n\}$. Absolute convergence (Lem 2.1.8) is what rules that out.

What convergence does NOT give you: properties. Pointwise convergence makes $f$ well-defined and nothing more. Continuity needs uniform convergence (Ex 2.2.4) — the pointwise limit of the continuous $f_n(x)=x^{n}$ on $[0,1]$ is discontinuous.

The slogan: convergence buys you the values; absolute convergence buys you order-independence; uniform convergence buys you the properties.
